Book of the day · 📖

The Hidden Mathematics of Life

Why does an elephant live so much longer than a mouse — and why do cities never die, but companies almost always do?

Geoffrey West · Scale · 2017 · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 13 →

The hook

Could one number connect a heartbeat, a city and a corporation?

A theoretical physicist turned his tools on life itself and found astonishingly simple laws hiding in the mess. The same mathematics that sets an animal’s lifespan, West argues, shapes how fast a city innovates — and why firms grow old and die.

6 / 13 →

The core idea

Growth follows universal ‘scaling laws’ — simple power laws that hold across organisms, cities and companies.

West shows that as size changes, key features don’t change in proportion but by predictable fractions. That single regularity, he believes, reveals something fundamental about the structure and organisation of living and social systems alike.

7 / 13 →
Finding 1 / 6 · The quarter-power law

Double the mass, add a quarter

An animal’s metabolic rate scales as its mass to the three-quarter power. Double a mammal’s mass and its time to maturity and its lifespan both rise by about a quarter, while its heart rate falls by a quarter. The same ‘1/4’ keeps appearing.

8 / 13 →
Finding 2 / 6 · Why growth stops

Economies of scale, then a ceiling

Because metabolism scales sublinearly with size, ever-bigger organisms enjoy economies of scale — but the energy supply eventually can’t keep up with the maintenance demands of all those cells. Growth slows and stops. Sublinear scaling means stable, bounded growth.

9 / 13 →
Finding 3 / 6 · The pace of life

Bigger means slower

The same laws set the tempo of biology. Larger animals live at a slower pace — hearts beating fewer times per minute, lives stretched over more years. The mouse burns fast and dies young; the elephant burns slow and lives long.

10 / 13 →
Finding 4 / 6 · Cities flip the rule

The 15 percent rule

Cities scale superlinearly. Double a city’s population and wages, patents, restaurants — but also crime and disease — all rise by roughly 15% per person. Even walking speed climbs. The pace of life speeds up, and growth becomes unbounded.

11 / 13 →
Finding 5 / 6 · Cities live, companies die

Firms age like animals

Companies, West finds, scale more like organisms than like cities — revenue playing the role of metabolism, expenses that of maintenance. So they mature, ossify and die: by the age of about 20 years, some 85% of companies have vanished through bankruptcy or takeover. Cities almost never do.

12 / 13 →
Finding 6 / 6 · The common cause

It’s all networks

The engine behind every scaling law, West argues, is networks — the branching systems that deliver resources. Blood vessels in a body; roads, railways and power lines in a city. Their shared geometry is why a city’s flows behave like the metabolism of a living thing.

13 / 13 ✓

The takeaway

“Cities are sustained by… roads, railways, and electrical lines that transport people, energy, and resources and whose flow is therefore a manifestation of the metabolism of the city.”

— Geoffrey West, Scale. Beneath the apparent chaos of life and economies lie a few strikingly simple laws — and knowing them changes how you see everything from an elephant to a metropolis.
