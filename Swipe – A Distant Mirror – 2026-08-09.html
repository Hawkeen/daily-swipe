A Distant Mirror

Plague, endless war and collapsing faith — a ‘violent, tormented’ century held up as a mirror to our own

Barbara W. Tuchman · A Distant Mirror: The Calamitous 14th Century · 1978 · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 14 →

The hook

What happens to a society when a third of its people die, the wars never end, and God seems to have left?

Tuchman went looking in the 14th century — and found an age at once glittering with cathedrals and chivalry and plunged into chaos and spiritual agony. She wrote it, in part, to understand her own century of collapsing certainties.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

Follow one life through the catastrophe, and the whole century comes into focus — and reflects our own.

Tuchman threads the maze by tracking a single well-connected French nobleman, Enguerrand VII de Coucy (1340–1395). Through his life she shows how chivalry, faith and everyday order were upended — and argues the tormented 14th century is a ‘distant mirror’ of the modern age.

7 / 14 →
Finding 1 / 6 · The lens

One man as a thread through the maze

With actors numbering in the hundreds, Tuchman keeps her focus by following Enguerrand de Coucy — the last of his line — as his life intersects the era’s great events. The nobleman becomes the mirror in which the whole century is reflected.

Finding 2 / 6 · The plague

A third of Europe, gone

The Black Death killed roughly a third of Europe’s people between 1347 and 1350, then returned again and again. Tuchman portrays a society terrorised and demoralised by mass death — the central shock from which every other calamity radiates.

9 / 14 →
Finding 3 / 6 · The war

The myth of the chivalrous knight dies

Against the backdrop of the Hundred Years’ War, Tuchman charts the decline of the feudal system and the ideal of chivalry. Knights clinging to a glamorous code of honour keep marching into slaughter — the gap between the ideal and the killing widening with every campaign.

10 / 14 →
Finding 4 / 6 · The Church

When there were two popes

The century saw the power of the Roman Catholic Church decline, culminating in the Papal Schism — rival popes excommunicating each other. The one institution meant to explain suffering and hold society together was itself split, deepening the era’s spiritual agony.

11 / 14 →
Finding 5 / 6 · The revolts

A brutalised, disintegrating age

Tuchman’s century is one of internecine wars, popular revolts and deprivation — a society, in her words, that is ‘violent, tormented, bewildered, suffering and disintegrating.’ The old order frays from below as much as from above.

12 / 14 →
Finding 6 / 6 · The mirror

Why ‘distant’ — and why a mirror

Tuchman wrote after the extraordinary shocks of the twentieth century, and she saw a rhyme: an age whose assumptions collapse, that survives plague and war and disorder, yet endures. The distance is six centuries; the reflection is unmistakably us.

13 / 14 →

The takeaway

“If our last decade or two of collapsing assumptions has been a period of unusual discomfort, it is reassuring to know that the human species has lived through worse before.”

— Barbara Tuchman. Her calamitous century is a warning and a consolation at once: societies can pass through plague, war and lost faith — battered, transformed, but still standing.
