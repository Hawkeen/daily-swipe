Episode of the day · 🎧

Not Even Close

Odd Lots (Bloomberg, 12 September 2026) — Robert Friedland on the world’s monumental shortage of copper

Hosts: Joe Weisenthal & Tracy Alloway · commodities · ~6 min read

5 / 14 →

Copper has surged another 15% this year. The man who found one of the largest copper deposits on earth says we are not even close to having enough for what is coming.

And mine supply is set to grow by roughly 1.4%.

6 / 14 →
The core idea

Structural, not cyclical

Copper demand has always been tightly linked to economic activity. What is different now, on Friedland’s account, is that it is in overdrive — driven by data centres, electric cars, and a global push to reindustrialise everywhere at once.

Friedland is founder and executive co-chairman of Ivanhoe Mines, so he is talking his book. But analysts now describe copper’s imbalance the same way he does: structural rather than cyclical — a gap that a normal price cycle will not close.

7 / 14 →
Finding 1 / 7

The price, in numbers

Copper has surged another 15% in 2026. Spot prices reached record highs above $14,000 a tonne at the start of the year.

A Reuters poll of 31 analysts put the median 2026 forecast at $11,975 a tonne — the highest consensus forecast ever recorded. Major investment banks cluster in a $10,000–13,000 band. The forecasters are not arguing about direction any more, only about how far.

8 / 14 →
Finding 2 / 7

The AI build-out is a metals story

AI data centres consumed 369 kilotonnes of copper in 2025 — a figure projected to exceed 500,000 tonnes a year by 2027.

Add electric vehicles, which need three to four times more copper than a petrol car, and the grid upgrades required to feed both. Every headline about model training and gigawatt campuses is also, somewhere upstream, an order for wire.

9 / 14 →
Finding 3 / 7

Supply barely moves

Against all that, mine supply growth for 2026 is estimated at roughly +1.4% — about 500,000 tonnes.

That asymmetry is the whole argument. Demand can be created by a purchase order; supply needs a discovery, a permit, a decade and a mine. Nothing about a high price this year adds metal to the market this year.

10 / 14 →
Finding 4 / 7

How big is the hole?

J.P. Morgan projects a refined copper shortfall of roughly 330,000 tonnes in 2026. The International Copper Study Group expects the market to shift to a 150,000-tonne deficit.

Either way the direction is the same: a market that was in surplus in 2025 flips into deficit in 2026. The spread between those two estimates — more than double — is itself a measure of how little anyone really knows.

11 / 14 →
Finding 5 / 7

Geology, then politics

Prices are also rising because copper is caught in the crossfire of trade wars and resource nationalism. The US is expected to place tariffs on the refined metal.

That converts a commodity into a policy instrument. Every producing country now has an obvious lever, and every consuming country an obvious vulnerability — which changes who builds what, where.

12 / 14 →
Finding 6 / 7

Surplus on the spreadsheet, scarcity in the warehouse

Massive US stockpiling, low freely available LME inventories, mine disruptions, strong Chinese consumption and tight concentrate supply can together create extreme physical tightness outside the US — even while aggregate global spreadsheets show a surplus.

This is the mechanism worth keeping. A global balance number can be perfectly accurate and still tell you nothing about whether a cable manufacturer in Europe can actually get metal next month.

13 / 14 →
Finding 7 / 7

The guest’s own hole in the ground

Ivanhoe Mines is accelerating plans for its Makoko District in the Democratic Republic of Congo, after expanding the giant discovery by 30% from its 2025 estimate to about 12 million tonnes of contained copper.

Worth holding both facts at once: this is a credible read of the market and an advertisement. The DRC location is also the point — the new copper is not where the new demand is.

14 / 14 ✓

The line to remember

“We’re not even close to having enough copper for what’s coming.”

— Robert Friedland, founder and executive co-chairman of Ivanhoe Mines, on Odd Lots, 12 September 2026

What to carry forward: demand for copper is now being written by software decisions — where to put a data centre, how fast to electrify — while supply is still governed by geology and permitting. Those two clocks run at completely different speeds, and the gap between them is the price.
