The Ending They Couldn’t Bear

Theatre · Shakespeare’s King Lear: a tragedy so bleak that England rewrote it with a happy ending — and kept that version on stage for 157 years.

Theatre · William Shakespeare · first recorded performance 26 Dec 1606 · ~6 min read

5 / 15 →

From 1681 to 1838, audiences who went to see King Lear watched Lear win his throne back and Cordelia get married.

Shakespeare had written something else: Lear entering “with Cordelia dead in his arms.” For more than a century and a half, the English stage simply played a different play.

6 / 15 →
Henry Fuseli, King Lear Admonishing Cordelia (c. 1784–90). Public domain via Wikimedia Commons.
The core idea

A play its own audiences kept trying to fix

The legend Shakespeare inherited restored Lear to his throne. His play does the opposite: the dying Edmund orders Cordelia’s execution, the messengers sent to stop it arrive too late, and Lear carries her in — “She’s dead as earth.”

What followed is a centuries-long argument — over which text is the real one, whether the ending can be endured, and whether the play is great at all.

Finding 1 / 7

The legend had a happy ending

The main source is Geoffrey of Monmouth’s 12th-century History of the Kings of Britain. There, Cordelia — married to a ruler in Gaul — invades, defeats her sisters’ husbands and restores Lear, who reigns three more years.

Only later, after his death, do her sisters’ sons capture her; she kills herself in prison. An anonymous play, King Leir, printed in 1605, may also have been a source.

Finding 2 / 7

There are two King Lears

The Quarto (1608) and the First Folio (1623) differ: about 285 Quarto lines are missing from the Folio, about 102 Folio lines are missing from the Quarto, plus many changed words and speech assignments.

Editors long blended them into one. In 1983, The Division of the Kingdoms (Taylor & Warren) argued the Folio is “a conscious revision by Shakespeare” — and the 1986 Oxford Complete Works printed both texts separately.

Finding 3 / 7

Nahum Tate’s repair job (1681)

Tate called Shakespeare’s play “a Heap of Jewels, unstrung and unpolisht.” His version adds a love story between Edgar and Cordelia, cuts the Fool entirely, and ends with Lear back on his throne and Cordelia marrying Edgar.

It worked: Betterton, Garrick and Kemble all became famous playing Tate’s Lear.

Finding 4 / 7

Samuel Johnson took Tate’s side

The great critic approved of the happy ending, noting that “Cordelia, from the time of Tate, has always retired with victory and felicity” — and adding: “In the present case the public has decided.”

The Romantics disagreed: Lamb, Hazlitt and Schlegel condemned Tate’s version.

Finding 5 / 7

The long way back to Shakespeare

1823: Edmund Kean briefly restored the tragic ending — then returned to Tate after a poor reception. 1838: William Charles Macready dropped Tate entirely at Covent Garden, bringing back the Fool and the tragic ending in a shortened text.

1845: Samuel Phelps finally staged the complete Shakespearean text.

Finding 6 / 7

Tolstoy vs. Orwell

In 1906 Tolstoy called Shakespeare “an insignificant, inartistic writer,” his fame an “epidemic suggestion” — and reserved his sharpest hostility for Lear.

Orwell’s reply, “Lear, Tolstoy and the Fool” (1947), noticed the irony: “Lear renounces his throne” but “expects everyone to continue treating him as a king” — much like Tolstoy, who gave up estate, title and copyrights. The real fight, Orwell said, was “the quarrel between the religious and the humanist attitude towards life.”

Finding 7 / 7

Lear meets Beckett

Jan Kott’s essay “King Lear, or Endgame,” in Shakespeare Our Contemporary, read the play through the Theatre of the Absurd. Kott’s essay and conversations with him lay behind Peter Brook’s 1962 Lear, with Paul Scofield as the King.

Not everyone was convinced. The playwright Edward Bond objected: “Shakespeare released history, Beckett tried to stop it.”

Why this matters in 2026

Lear is on stage this autumn

At Manchester’s Royal Exchange (2 Oct – 15 Nov 2026), David Threlfall returns after 27 years to play Lear in Matthew Dunster’s adaptation — part of the theatre’s 50th-anniversary season, billed as “a searing portrait of a king unable to distinguish truth from lies.”

In Chicago, Babes With Blades opens its King Lear at Theater Wit on 22 October, its artistic director quoting the play: “’Tis the time’s plague when madmen lead the blind.”
