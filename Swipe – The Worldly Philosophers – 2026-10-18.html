Book of the day · 📖

The Worldly Philosophers

The lives, times and ideas of the great economic thinkers — told as biography, not as equations.

Robert L. Heilbroner · first published 1953 · 7th edition 1999
~368 pp. · nearly four million copies sold · ~6 min read

5 / 15 →
The hook

Why would a 1953 book about long-dead economists sell nearly four million copies?

It is the second-best-selling economics text of all time, behind only Paul Samuelson’s Economics. The New York Times called it “all the economic lore most general readers conceivably could want to know, served up with a flourish.”

6 / 15 →
The core idea

Economics as a story of people and their times

Heilbroner surveys the lives, times and ideas of the major economic thinkers — Smith, Malthus and Ricardo, the utopian socialists, Marx, the Victorians, Veblen, Keynes and Schumpeter — and treats each as a product of the society he was trying to explain.

His chapter titles do the arguing: “The Wonderful World of Adam Smith”, “The Gloomy Presentiments of Parson Malthus and David Ricardo”, “The Inexorable System of Karl Marx”, “The Savage Society of Thorstein Veblen”, “The Heresies of John Maynard Keynes”, “The Contradictions of Joseph Schumpeter”.

Finding 1 / 7

Adam Smith: self-interest, harnessed

Smith, who taught at Glasgow and published The Theory of Moral Sentiments in 1759, argued in The Wealth of Nations (1776) that self-interest and competition regulate markets and generate wealth.

In Heilbroner’s telling Smith envisioned a self-regulating market where prices and output are set by demand and competition — while warning against monopolies, the force that could break that mechanism.

Finding 2 / 7

The gloomy pair: Malthus and Ricardo

Malthus’s Essay on the Principle of Population (1798) argued that population would outrun food supply, bringing recurring poverty and suffering.

Ricardo, the great abstract analyst, held that rent flowed disproportionately to landlords — the interests of landowners always opposed to every other class — an argument tied to the Corn Laws debates. His abstraction gave a name to a danger: the “Ricardian vice”, when a model stops reflecting reality.

Finding 3 / 7

The utopians: building the better world

Robert Owen ran New Lanark, proving humane factory conditions were possible, and proposed “Villages of Cooperation”. His New Harmony project in America collapsed — but his ideas fed the cooperative movement and the Rochdale Pioneers. Saint-Simon wanted society organised by productivity; Fourier, communal phalanstères.

John Stuart Mill argued for reform within capitalism, quoted by Heilbroner: “The distribution of wealth depends on the laws and customs of society.”

Finding 4 / 7

Marx: the inexorable system

With Engels, Marx wrote the Communist Manifesto amid the revolutions of 1848. In his view economic structures drive political and cultural life, history moves through class struggle, and profit is surplus value — unpaid labour.

He predicted recurring crises that would sharpen class conflict and end in socialism. Readers single out Heilbroner’s portrait of Marx’s eccentric life alongside the prescience of Das Kapital’s observations.

Finding 5 / 7

The underworld of economics

In “The Victorian World and the Underworld of Economics”, Heilbroner rescues heretics outside the respectable mainstream. One is John Hobson, who saw capitalism turning into imperialism: industrial nations using colonies as “dumping grounds” for their products.

The example readers remember: India and British textiles.

Finding 6 / 7

Veblen: the savage society

In The Theory of the Leisure Class, Thorstein Veblen argued that conspicuous consumption ties social status to wealth rather than merit.

In The Theory of Business Enterprise he went further: businessmen often hinder production for the sake of profit — and engineers and technicians, he predicted, would gain influence.

Finding 7 / 7

Keynes and Schumpeter: two answers to the slump

Keynes, already famous for attacking the Treaty of Versailles in The Economic Consequences of the Peace, argued in the General Theory that governments should spend when private investment falters.

Schumpeter saw entrepreneurs’ innovations driving profit and boom-and-bust cycles, and in Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy foresaw capitalism sliding into a socialism run by elites, not the proletariat — a darker view than Keynes’s 1930 essay “Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren”.

The author

A worldly philosopher himself

Heilbroner was born in New York in 1919, graduated summa cum laude from Harvard in 1940, and during the war worked at the Office of Price Control under John Kenneth Galbraith. After a brief spell as a banker he joined the New School for Social Research, where he became Norman Thomas Professor of Economics in 1971 and taught the history of economic thought for more than two decades. He died in 2005.

The 1999 edition’s new last chapter, “The End of the Worldly Philosophy?”, is grim about the state of economics — and hopes for a “reborn worldly philosophy” that takes in the social side of capitalism.

“If one could divine the nature of the economic forces in the world, one could foretell the future.”

— Robert L. Heilbroner, The Worldly Philosophers

One thing to carry: every great economic theory was an answer to the crisis of its own day — Smith’s new markets, Ricardo’s landlords, Marx’s factories, Keynes’s Depression. Ask of any modern model which crisis it was built to explain.
