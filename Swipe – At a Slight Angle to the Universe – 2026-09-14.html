Creative topic · 🎨

At a Slight Angle to the Universe

Constantine P. Cavafy (1863–1933) — Alexandria

Poetry · modern Greek · ~6 min read

5 / 15 →

He never published a book. He printed poems on loose sheets and handed them to whoever he thought might care.

He is now one of the most translated poets of the twentieth century — and the first book appeared two years after he died.

6 / 15 →
The core idea

History as a mask for the present

Cavafy’s poems are concise, intimate evocations of real or literary figures and milieux from Greek culture. He drew on personal experience plus a deep knowledge of history, especially the Hellenistic era — and many poems are pseudo-historical, or seemingly historical, or accurately but quirkily historical.

His defining themes: uncertainty about the future, sensual pleasure, the moral character and psychology of individuals, homosexuality, and a fatalistic existential nostalgia. His most important poems were written after his fortieth birthday.

Finding 1 / 7

A temporary clerk for thirty years

Born in Alexandria in 1863 to Greek parents; his father died in 1870, and the family moved between Liverpool and London before financial trouble in the Long Depression brought them back to Alexandria in 1877.

Because of his foreign citizenship he could only be hired as a temporary clerk in the British-run Egyptian Ministry of Public Works — a post he then renewed annually for thirty years. He quit in 1922 and characterised the act as liberation.

Finding 2 / 7

A canon of exactly 154

The canon is 154 poems — those Cavafy himself put into circulation in two “volumes” and ten “collections”, plus one unpublished but assumed ready for printing at his death.

Around it: 75 ‘hidden’ poems, found finished in his archive or in friends’ hands and not published until 1968; 37 repudiated poems he published and then renounced; and 30 unfinished ones. He curated his own reputation more tightly than most editors would dare.

Finding 3 / 7

Publishing by refusal

He consistently refused to publish his work in books. He circulated it through local newspapers and magazines, or printed it himself on broadsheets and gave it away to anyone who might be interested — between 1891 and 1904, only to close friends.

Any acclaim came from within the Greek community of Alexandria until a favourable 1903 review by Gregorios Xenopoulos introduced him to mainland literary circles. The first book, Poiēmata, appeared posthumously in Alexandria in 1935.

Finding 4 / 7

A language nobody else was writing

His style mixed the formal Katharevousa with everyday Demotic Greek — and drew the criticism of Kostis Palamas, the leading mainland poet of the era, and his followers, who favoured the simplest Demotic. Like the ancient Alexandrians, he built an artificial poetic language of deliberate archaisms and colloquialisms.

His mature form was free iambic: lines rarely rhyme and usually run 10 to 17 syllables. And when rhyme does appear, it usually signals irony. He was a perfectionist who obsessively refined every single line.

Finding 5 / 7

Three categories, on his own instruction

Cavafy left instructions that his poems be classified as historical, philosophical, and hedonistic or sensual.

The historical poems centre on the Hellenistic era with Alexandria at the focus, plus Helleno-Roman antiquity and Byzantium. The periods he picks are mostly of decline and decadence, his heroes facing the final end — he describes Actium from the point of view of a pedlar knocked over by the crowd, and sets a swaggering poem about Alexander’s triumphs at the moment the Greek kingdoms are about to fall to Rome.

Finding 6 / 7

‘Waiting for the Barbarians’, 1904

A city-state in decline; its population and its legislators wait all day for the barbarians to arrive. Night falls, and the barbarians have not come. The closing turn asks what is to become of everyone now — because those people were, in their way, a kind of solution.

It has kept working on other writers ever since: Dino Buzzati’s The Tartar Steppe (1940), Julien Gracq’s The Opposing Shore (1951), and J. M. Coetzee’s Waiting for the Barbarians (1980) all take the same premise — the enemy a state needs in order to know what it is.

Finding 7 / 7

‘Ithaca’, 1911

Written in 1911 and often considered his best-known poem, it takes Odysseus’s homeward journey from the Odyssey and inverts the moral: it is the destination that produces the journey. The traveller should set out with hope and not hurry.

At the end you may find the island has no more riches to give you — but, in the poem’s formulation, Ithaca gave you the marvellous journey. Frank H. T. Rhodes built his last commencement address as president of Cornell, in 1995, around it.

Why this matters in 2026

The archive opened, and so did the life

Alexandrian Sphinx: The Hidden Life of Constantine Cavafy by Peter Jeffreys and Gregory Jusdanis (Simon & Schuster) is the first English-language biography of Cavafy since Robert Liddell’s. It was shortlisted for the Anglo-Hellenic League’s Runciman Award; the Guardian called it “a deeply researched and engaging biography”, the Literary Review “a nuanced and original portrait”.

It rests on material that only recently became usable. The Onassis Foundation acquired the Cavafy Archive in 2012, launched its digital collection — freely accessible to all — in March 2019, and opened a dedicated physical Cavafy Archive space in Athens in November 2023. Jeffreys sits on the archive’s academic committee.

The line to remember

“A Greek gentleman in a straw hat, standing absolutely motionless at a slight angle to the universe.”

— E. M. Forster on Cavafy, Pharos and Pharillon (1923). Forster, who called him simply “The Poet”, first brought his work to English readers that year.

What to carry forward: Cavafy is the standing argument against publication as the measure of a writing life. Thirty years of clerking, a few hundred broadsheets pressed into the right hands, 154 poems kept and the rest withheld — and the work outlived every literary establishment that ignored it.
