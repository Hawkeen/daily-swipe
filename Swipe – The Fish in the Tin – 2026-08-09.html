Episode of the day · 🎧

The Fish in the Tin

How a humble sardine travels from ocean to can — and becomes a premium, climate-exposed business

Odd Lots (Bloomberg) · with Becca Millstein of Fishwife · 7 August 2026 · ~5 min read

Swipe →

5 / 14 →

The hook

How did the cheapest thing on the shelf become a status symbol?

On Odd Lots, Fishwife’s Becca Millstein traces the journey of a sardine from the sea to a designer tin — and reveals a supply chain squeezed by climate change and geopolitics behind the ‘seacuterie’ trend.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

A tin of fish is a whole economy in miniature — branding, boom demand, a fragile catch and shifting geopolitics, all in one can.

Tinned fish has flipped from budget staple to premium good. The episode uses one artisanal brand to show how a product positions itself in today’s media market — and how exposed it is to the ocean and to trade policy upstream.

7 / 14 →
Finding 1 / 5 · The boom

From budget snack to premium good

Tinned fish transformed from a budget product into a premium category. It took off during COVID, and shoppers now reach for high-end brands. Between ‘seacuterie’ boards, tinned-fish tastings and boutique shops, a long-standing snack has a fresh lease on life.

8 / 14 →
Finding 2 / 5 · The brand

Fishwife, born in 2020

Becca Millstein founded Fishwife in 2020, positioning it as a distinct, artisanal product — closer to what you’d find in a European grocery than a US pantry staple. Much of the discussion is about how a new brand carves out identity in today’s crowded media market.

9 / 14 →
Finding 3 / 5 · The premium

Why a tin can cost more

In European specialty retail, processors that invest in vintage-dated tins, single-catch labelling and chef collaborations can capture a documented 15–20% price premium. Supermarkets are widening shelf space for premium lines as sustainable, ready-to-eat seafood goes mainstream.

10 / 14 →
Finding 4 / 5 · The catch

A supply chain at the mercy of the sea

Beyond scaling, the business is shaped by climate change and geopolitics. Sardine landings in Morocco — a linchpin supplier — fell about 46%, from 965,000 tonnes in 2022 to 525,000 in 2024, reflecting stock pressure and climate variability, and driving price spikes.

11 / 14 →
Finding 5 / 5 · The market

A $15-billion tin

This is no niche. Industry estimates put the global tinned-fish market near $14.4 billion in 2025, rising toward roughly $19.6 billion by 2031 — growth driven by demand for sustainable, premium, ready-to-eat seafood across developed and emerging markets alike.

12 / 14 →
Why this matters in 2026

The tin meets the trade barrier

From 1 February 2026, Morocco banned exports of frozen sardines to protect domestic supply and prices — a resource that feeds not only its own canneries but a large share of Europe’s. As the premium category keeps booming, the raw fish behind it is getting scarcer and more contested.

13 / 14 →

The takeaway

Pull the tab on a designer tin and you open a supply chain running from a warming sea, through a scarce national catch and an export ban, to a shelf where scarcity is sold as luxury.

Classic Odd Lots: an everyday object turns out to be a lesson in branding, climate risk and geopolitics all at once.
