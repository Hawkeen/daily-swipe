Four Witnesses, No Truth

Akira Kurosawa’s Rashomon (1950): one death in a forest, four accounts that cannot all be true — and the film that put Japanese cinema on the world stage.

Film · Daiei, Tokyo premiere 25 Aug 1950 · 88 min · ~6 min read

5 / 15 →

A samurai is dead. Four people tell what happened. Their stories do not match.

Kurosawa’s three assistant directors asked him to explain the script. He told them the film was about “the impossibility of truly understanding human psychology.” Two went off to reread it. The third was eventually asked to resign.

6 / 15 →
The core idea

Not a whodunit — a study in self-serving memory

Rashomon stages one event — a samurai’s death and his wife’s assault in a forest — through four conflicting testimonies, framed by three men sheltering from rain under a ruined city gate.

Kurosawa’s own reading, as he gave it to his assistants: “Human beings are unable to be honest with themselves about themselves. They cannot talk about themselves without embellishing.” He called the film “a strange picture scroll that is unrolled and displayed by the ego.”

Finding 1 / 7

Two short stories, one film

The plot and characters come from Ryūnosuke Akutagawa’s 1922 short story “In a Grove”; the title and the framing story at the gate come from his earlier story “Rashōmon”.

The script began as a draft by Shinobu Hashimoto based on “In a Grove”; Kurosawa added “Rashōmon” and co-wrote the screenplay with him.

Finding 2 / 7

Four testimonies, four versions

The witnesses: the bandit Tajōmaru (Toshiro Mifune), the wife Masago (Machiko Kyō), the dead samurai (Masayuki Mori) — speaking through a medium — and the woodcutter (Takashi Shimura).

Each account contradicts the others. Even the dead man’s testimony, delivered from beyond the grave, is not treated as neutral.

Finding 3 / 7

Pointing the camera at the sun

Filming sunlight directly was considered taboo; Kurosawa wanted it anyway, and cinematographer Kazuo Miyagawa agreed to take the risk. In the forest, where electric lights couldn’t be used, eight mirrors bounced sunlight onto the actors, and leaves on a net overhead cast shadows across their faces.

Miyagawa chose Fujifilm stock — considered inferior to Kodak — for its strong contrast. Kurosawa, who had studied French avant-garde silent films, described the result as a “play of light and shadow”: “I like silent pictures and I always have.”

Finding 4 / 7

Three sets, half a roof, black rain

Only three sets were built: the gate, the forest, and the police courtyard. The gate was so immense, Kurosawa said, that “a complete roof would have buckled the support pillars” — so the crew built half a roof and justified it as dilapidation.

Rain from fire hoses didn’t register on camera against the sky, so the water was tinted with black ink. Under the gate, critic Alexander Sesonske wrote, “time hangs heavily, dead, empty, idle”; in the forest, “time is charged with passion and anxiety.”

Finding 5 / 7

A grammar of rule-breaking

Sesonske’s Criterion essay tracks the formal audacity: the woodcutter’s walk into the forest runs “sixteen shots of continuous varied movement while the story waits in abeyance”.

Kurosawa “breaks the 180-degree rule, thus reversing spatial relationships,” uses camera movement as “cinematic punctuation,” and revives the wipe — which “becomes and remains for Kurosawa an element of style.” The three men at the gate act as “a chorus commenting on the action in the clearing.”

Finding 6 / 7

Rejected at home, crowned in Venice

Toho declined the project; Daiei first turned it down before agreeing. After a preview, Daiei president Masaichi Nagata said: “I don’t really get it, but it’s a noble photograph.” Japanese companies objected it wasn’t representative of Japanese cinema and preferred Ozu.

Giuliana Stramigioli of Italifilm recommended it to Venice anyway. It won the Golden Lion in 1951 and an honorary Academy Award on 20 March 1952. Japanese critics still disliked it; Kurosawa believed his countrymen distrusted a Japanese film that Western audiences enjoyed.

Finding 7 / 7

A baby, the rain stops — and a new word

The woodcutter finally admits he lied and had hidden the dagger. The film ends with him taking an abandoned baby home as the rain stops. Sesonske notes that the Western music dominant through the film gives way to traditional Japanese music at the close.

The title outlived the film: the “Rashomon effect” now names any situation where several people give contradictory accounts of the same event — as Time Out New York put it, the title “has become synonymous with its chief narrative conceit.”

Why this matters in 2026

Still on the big screen, 76 years on

In 2026 the Academy Museum in Los Angeles ran Darkness and Humanity: The Complete Akira Kurosawa, screening Rashomon in 35mm on 29 March; Brisbane’s Queensland Art Gallery | Gallery of Modern Art showed it in February.

Its museum blurb still leads with the same legacy: “A samurai’s death unfolds through conflicting witness accounts, pioneering the ‘Rashomon effect.’”
