The Tax You Pay in Hours

“This Is Why People Hate the Government”

The Ezra Klein Show · 25 August 2026
with Annie Lowrey, author of The Time Tax (Ecco, 2026) · ~6 min read

5 / 15 →

How much of your life do you spend waiting — in line, on hold, filling in forms — for something that is already yours?

“I really saw this as a tax, because there’s this understanding that, with the government, we should be getting what’s ours,” Lowrey says.

6 / 15 →
The core argument

A tax nobody legislated

The “time tax” is Lowrey’s term for the paperwork, aggravation and mental effort imposed on citizens when they access the rights and benefits that are supposed to be theirs.

For those applying for financial aid, housing assistance, unemployment, disability or Medicaid, the administrative burden is worse — and the stakes are infinitely higher. The question she keeps asking: how much time, effort and emotional strain do you have to give up to receive something you are supposed to have?

The agency that doesn’t exist

If Washington were to create a Department of Bureaucracy to take on the paperwork it currently shunts to citizens, the agency would require 5.9 million employees.

That would make it four times the size of the active-duty military — an enormous public workload that has simply been moved off the government’s books and onto private lives.

Finding 2 / 7

It falls hardest where need is greatest

The programmes targeted at the most at-risk Americans — SSI, used by very low-income seniors with disabilities; TANF, used mainly by very low-income mothers of small children; WIC, nutrition support for low-income mothers and their kids — “tend to come with really significant time burdens”.

SSI, for instance, makes claimants comply with an asset test: exactly how much money is in your bank account, and whether you have “any little pockets saved away somewhere”. Lowrey’s view is that those programmes should be easier, not harder.

Finding 3 / 7

Even the benefits you paid for

“Unemployment insurance is a contributory benefit. You have to be paying into a trust fund in order to get it,” Lowrey notes.

And yet some people never collect — either because they cannot figure out how to complete the paperwork, or because “the state, frankly, can’t figure out how to deal with all of the paperwork that it’s receiving”.

Finding 4 / 7

Social Security shows it doesn’t have to be like this

It is close to the biggest programme the US government runs. Its overhead cost is 1% and the payment error rate is extremely low.

And it is not because the task is easy: the agency has to monitor your income and employment across your entire working life. “They’ve already done all of the work, but that was a lot of work in the background. They just don’t impose it on people.”

Finding 5 / 7

Voting is a time tax too

Americans vote on a Tuesday because, in an agrarian society, Sunday was for church, market days had to be avoided, and voters might travel a day on foot or by horse. Congress settled on Tuesday in the 1840s — and it is not a holiday.

The result, Lowrey argues, is low voter participation, which means the voting public is not representative of the actual American public. Vote by mail had started to change that.

Finding 6 / 7

The bill is paid in trust

“When the government treats us like this, we don’t trust it. And if we don’t trust it, we’re not going to trust it to do more and better things for our life.”

And it does not stop at the state: “I think that it leads us not to trust each other, too.” The time tax, on this reading, is one of the machines that manufactures a low-trust society.

Finding 7 / 7

It is a design choice — so it can be undesigned

Lowrey traces a history of administrative burdens in the United States from the colonial era to today, revealing how they were historically a tool of discrimination. Countries from Estonia to Vietnam have made reducing them a priority.

Her prescription cuts across the usual argument about big versus small government: “you can make the government smaller by making the rules easier, giving the government a little more budget to update these systems and giving it the express goal of reducing burden on citizens and taxpayers.”

Why this matters in 2026

Paperwork is doing policy work right now

New paperwork and work requirements have already cut millions of Americans from food assistance: over 4 million people are no longer receiving food aid amid sweeping changes to SNAP — and the numbers are still growing.

The voting side of the argument is live too: the Postal Service is appealing an order blocking the plan behind the push to restrict mail voting, reported on 26 August 2026.

Lowrey’s book landed on 25 August 2026 — the same day the episode ran — and she took it to NPR’s Fresh Air on 31 August.

The line to remember

“Food assistance should not be a program for low-income, at-risk Americans who might not be able to eat enough, who are also good at paperwork.”

— Annie Lowrey, Fresh Air (NPR), 31 August 2026

Try this: the next time a policy is described as “targeted”, ask what the targeting costs in hours — and who has those hours to spend. Eligibility written on paper and eligibility in practice are two different programmes.
