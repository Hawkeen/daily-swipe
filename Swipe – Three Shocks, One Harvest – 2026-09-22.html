Episode of the day · 🎧

Three Shocks, One Harvest

War, a blocked strait and a super El Niño are converging on the world’s food supply — at the exact moment nobody is in charge.

“The geopolitics of global hunger” · Mark Leonard’s World in 30 Minutes (ECFR)
11 September 2026 · with Cary Fowler and Jim O’Brien · recorded 9 September · ~6 min read

5 / 15 →

Almost 2.2 billion people already face food insecurity. Global food prices are already almost 75% above their 1999 lows.

Now three separate shocks are arriving on top of that — at once.

6 / 15 →
The core idea

A convergence, plus an absence

Cary Fowler — founder and president of the Food Security Leadership Council and former US global food security envoy — and Jim O’Brien, former US assistant secretary of state for European and Eurasian affairs, argue that three features of today’s unordered world are converging into a devastating food crisis.

The second half of the argument is the harder one: the looming catastrophe coincides with an absence of political leadership, including in multilateral institutions. In 2022 a coalition convened by UN secretary-general António Guterres curbed a comparable crisis. Today, they argue, even that convening power is missing.

7 / 15 →
Finding 1 / 7

Shock one: both breadbaskets are under fire

Ukraine and Russia together generate 30% of the world’s wheat exports, 20% of its corn, 60% of its sunflower oil and — from Russia alone — 20% of its fertiliser. They are the two bright spots among big global agricultural producers, and both are now disrupted.

Russian attacks on Ukraine’s highly exposed grain and port infrastructure have cut Ukrainian exports by 75%, with more damage done in August. Ukrainian strikes on Russian port facilities and ships in the Black Sea and Sea of Azov have taken over 90% of Russia’s grain export capacity offline.

Finding 2 / 7

Shock two: the strait that carries the fertiliser

The Strait of Hormuz remains blocked. It is the largest single conduit for the nitrogen fertilisers most directly linked to yield, and the export route for about half the global supply of sulphur, a vital component of phosphate fertilisers.

The upshot: about one third of global seaborne fertilisers cannot reach fields. The pain falls hardest on smallholder farmers in sub-Saharan Africa, where fertiliser prices are highest, and in south Asia.

Finding 3 / 7

Shock three: a ‘super El Niño’

This year’s El Niño — a sporadic warming of the Pacific with knock-on effects worldwide — could be the most drastic on record. A “super El Niño” would bring heat and drought to southern Africa and significantly reduce food production across south and south-east Asia.

Wheat production is already expected to fall by 3% globally in 2026–27, with much sharper drops in particular producers: 8% in the EU, 12% in Australia, 19% in Argentina. Rice prices would rise too.

Finding 4 / 7

The damage that outlasts the shock

The ripple effects may last years. In states like Malawi, rising prices and exhausted government subsidies are deterring fertiliser use — which depresses future production, not just this season’s.

And El Niño events are historically associated with protectionism. India has imposed export restrictions on rice that hit countries in south-east Asia; restrictions raise prices, lower margins and curb investment in future output. The shock teaches producers to hoard, and the hoarding makes the next shock worse.

Finding 5 / 7

Why the 2022 fix will not simply repeat

In 2022 a UN- and Turkey-facilitated arrangement kept Russian and Ukrainian grain and fertiliser moving through alternative routes, helped along by US and EU agricultural aid in sub-Saharan Africa.

In 2026 the obstacles are both logistical and diplomatic. War damage to infrastructure and low water levels in the Danube make it harder to move grain out of the region. The UN is approaching a leadership transition, the US has turned against the organisation, and there is no global focal point for action.

12 / 15 →
Finding 6 / 7

What they actually propose

A coalition convened fast by European leaders — including the UK and Ukraine if it wishes — joining states influential with the combatants (India, China, Turkey especially) to states most exposed (sub-Saharan Africa, south and south-east Asia), with a core group of middle powers: Brazil, Egypt, Kazakhstan, Mexico, Turkey.

Four elements: food relief plus technical assistance on drought-resistant agriculture and soil fertility, headed by an adviser to the UN secretary-general; funding, including a CGIAR-led sustainable productivity push with a $500m budget over three years; a partial Russia–Ukraine ceasefire covering grain ships, ports and inputs; and an agreement with Iran letting fertiliser transit Hormuz, overseen by an expert-level group like the one used in 2022.

13 / 15 →
Finding 7 / 7

The case that it would work

The technical assistance is not speculative. A CGIAR-led programme reached almost 6 million African farmers in 2023–25; in Malawi it delivered maize yields 64% higher than the national average.

The money is the harder part. The World Food Programme’s conservative pre-El Niño budget needs for 2026 are only 34% met so far. The authors frame the whole initiative as a “no regrets” effort: even if the combatants take time to join, supporting farmers and global trade improves the food situation anyway.

14 / 15 →
Why this matters in 2026

The window they named is this week

Fowler, O’Brien and ECFR’s Jana Kobzová published the underlying commentary on 7 September 2026 with a specific deadline attached: the coalition should bring leaders, or at least ministers, together on the margins of the UN General Assembly in the week of 21 September.

They are explicit that this requires multiple states to cross their red lines, that both wars driving food prices may escalate first, and that Russia especially would be reluctant. Their answer is that the affected states are numerous enough, and the fix cheap enough, that the attempt is worth making even if it fails.

“But its very defiance, its assertion that common action is still possible, makes it all the more valuable.”

One thing to carry: food security here is not an agricultural problem with a geopolitical backdrop. Two of the three shocks are chokepoints — the Black Sea and Hormuz — and chokepoints are diplomatic objects, not agronomic ones.
