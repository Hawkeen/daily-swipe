Book of the day · 📖

Being Starved to Death

How Mao’s Great Leap Forward became the deadliest famine on record — reconstructed from the Party’s own archives.

Frank Dikötter · Mao’s Great Famine: The History of China’s Most Devastating Catastrophe, 1958–62
Walker & Co. / Bloomsbury, 2010 · 448 pp. · BBC Samuel Johnson Prize 2011 · ~6 min read

5 / 15 →
The hook

“Take Pol Pot and multiply by ten and you get close to the horror.”

That is how Frank Dikötter summed up his subject when the book won the 2011 Samuel Johnson Prize, the UK’s largest award for non-fiction. His estimate: at least 45 million premature deaths in four years.

6 / 15 →
The core thesis

Not an accident. A decision.

Dikötter argues that the Great Chinese Famine of 1958–62 was a man-made catastrophe caused by decisions of the top leadership in Beijing — not a natural disaster, and not a problem officials were unaware of. It was the product of Mao’s campaign to accelerate industrial production.

His summary is blunt: “In most cases the party knew very well that it was starving its own people to death.”

7 / 15 →
Finding 1 / 7

A race to overtake Britain

After Khrushchev claimed the Soviet Union would overtake the United States economically, Mao promised that China would surpass Britain’s steel output within fifteen years. The Great Leap Forward followed.

Villagers were pushed into collective farms and people’s communes, made to give up private property, and pulled off the fields for vast irrigation projects. The plan also aimed to repay China’s Soviet debts early.

8 / 15 →
Finding 2 / 7

Melting the farm to make steel

Farm equipment was thrown into makeshift backyard furnaces. In Henan alone, the book records, “more than 140,000 tonnes of farming tools had been thrown into the backyard furnaces.”

The destruction went far beyond tools: by Dikötter’s account 30–40 percent of rural housing was demolished, and in some provinces up to half of all trees were cut down.

9 / 15 →
Finding 3 / 7

Quotas built on lies

Officials inflated targets and promised more than they could deliver. After the summer of 1959, setting a low target was treated as sabotage. Cadres assumed farmers were hiding grain — and seized it.

At a meeting in Shanghai in March 1959, Dikötter writes, Mao ordered grain procurement of up to one-third of the harvest. Famine was widespread by 1959 and worse in 1960.

10 / 15 →
Finding 4 / 7

Exporting food during a famine

Beijing raised procurement quotas in the countryside to pay for imports — and kept exporting food to allies, along with economic aid and low-interest loans, while its own villagers starved.

After the 1960 Sino-Soviet split, prestige came first and repayment of Soviet loans was accelerated. Mao even asked whether meat should be banned domestically “and that all of it should be exported.”

11 / 15 →
Finding 5 / 7

Hunger as a weapon

Most victims starved, but Dikötter counts some 2.5 million people tortured or summarily executed, and one to three million suicides. Local cadres controlled the food supply and could withhold it from anyone they disapproved of; the old, the sick and the weak were treated as expendable.

In the Xinyang region more than a million people died in 1960, 67,000 of them clubbed to death. In Guangshan county, about one in four of half a million inhabitants died.

12 / 15 →
Finding 6 / 7

Silencing the alarm

In the summer of 1959 senior officials raised the alarm about the famine. Mao treated this as a challenge to his power: the dissenters were purged, and an anti-party campaign that followed killed thousands.

Only in the spring of 1961 did Liu Shaoqi see the devastation in Hunan for himself.

Finding 7 / 7

A window that has closed

The book rests on four years in newly opened provincial, county and city archives; Dikötter was one of very few historians allowed in. He worked with Zhou Xun, whose documents and survivor testimonies the book also uses. Beijing’s central archives stayed shut.

Access tightened around the 2008 Olympics. “I don’t think it would be possible to research the book now,” he said — and noted an irony: “So through some very bizarre twists, that catastrophe gives the party some credit today.”

The debate

How many — and how deliberate?

The 45 million figure is contested. Other estimates include Yang Jisheng’s 36 million, Chen Yizi’s 43 million and Yu Xiguang’s 55 million; government figures from the 1980s point to about 30 million. Critics such as Ó Gráda and Walder dispute the baseline mortality Dikötter used.

Others question his reading of a remark by Mao about letting half the people die, which Garnaut and Bernstein take as hyperbole. One reviewer concludes Mao’s guilt lay in ignorance, arrogance and refusal to admit error — a different kind of evil, not a lesser one.

15 / 15 ✓

“There is a difference between starving to death and being starved to death.”

— Frank Dikötter, answering comparisons with earlier famines (as quoted by the historian Wemheuer)

One thing to carry: the Great Leap was a planning failure turned into mass death by politics — quotas no one dared lower, warnings that were punished, and grain shipped abroad for prestige.
