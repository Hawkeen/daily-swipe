Book of the day · 📖

The Lands in Between

Bloodlands: Europe Between Hitler and Stalin — Timothy Snyder, 2010

History · Eastern Europe, 1933–1945 · ~6 min read

5 / 14 →

Fourteen million people were murdered in one stretch of Europe between 1933 and 1945 — and the majority of them never saw a death camp.

They were shot, starved or gassed where they lived, in the lands between Berlin and Moscow.

6 / 14 →
The core idea

One history, not two

Snyder presents the mass murders committed by the Nazi and Stalinist regimes as two aspects of a single history — defined by the time and the place where they happened: between Germany and Russia, while Hitler and Stalin both held power.

The argument goes further than adding up two sets of atrocities. In the bloodlands the two regimes, despite conflicting goals, interacted — each raising the suffering above what either would have inflicted on its own.

7 / 14 →
Finding 1 / 7

The unit of analysis is a map

The bloodlands run from central Poland through Ukraine, Belarus and the Baltic states to western Russia. Between 1933 and 1945 an estimated 14 million non-combatants were killed there.

Nazi Germany was responsible for about two thirds of the total. Snyder’s method is to hold the territory fixed and let the regimes move across it — which is why the book looks nothing like a history of Germany or a history of the USSR.

8 / 14 →
Finding 2 / 7

It begins with farmland

Snyder roots the explanation for political killing in agriculture. Stalin wanted to collectivise the farmers; Hitler wanted to eliminate them, so that Germans could colonise the land.

In 1928 Stalin announced the first of the Five Year Plans, mandating the forced collectivisation of agricultural land. The same black soil that made the region worth taking made it worth emptying.

9 / 14 →
Finding 3 / 7

1932–33: the Ukrainian famine

Snyder estimates that the Soviet starvation of Ukrainians in 1932–33 killed 3.3 million people.

This is the first of the book’s great set pieces — and it sits eight years before the mass shootings and gas chambers that most readers associate with the region. The killing in the bloodlands did not begin in 1941.

10 / 14 →
Finding 4 / 7

1937–38: the Great Terror, seen from the edge

Snyder estimates the Great Terror of 1937–38 killed about 300,000 people in the bloodlands — and that the victims were mostly Poles and Ukrainians.

That is a different Terror from the familiar one. The show trials of Old Bolsheviks in Moscow are the version the West remembers; the national operations against ethnic minorities on the western border killed far more people.

11 / 14 →
Finding 5 / 7

The Hunger Plan

The Nazis operated a Hunger Plan, designed by Herbert Backe, under which food was routinely denied to civilians in German-occupied territories.

Under it, 4.2 million people in the Soviet Union were starved to death — mostly Ukrainians, Belarusians and Russians. Starvation here was not a by-product of war or bad logistics. It was the policy.

12 / 14 →
Finding 6 / 7

The prisoners nobody counts

About 57% of Soviet prisoners of war were killed in German captivity — against roughly 4% of American and British POWs. Three million Soviet soldiers starved to death in Nazi camps.

Hold those two percentages next to each other and the point of the book comes into focus: the same war, fought by the same army, was a different event depending on which side of the map you were captured on.

13 / 14 →
Finding 7 / 7

A contested book — in both directions

Jews make up 5.4 million of the 14 million victims, and much of Western historiography had focused narrowly on the Holocaust — the gap Bloodlands was written to fill.

But the book is argued over. Snyder declines to call the Ukrainian famine the Holodomor and uses low estimates for Stalin-era deaths compared with most other historians, concluding that the widely held assumption that more people died under Stalin’s regime is untrue. Leading scholars — Mace, Conquest, Snyder, Naimark, Applebaum — have all concluded the famine was genocide; the German Bundestag has debated applying that label.

14 / 14 ✓

The line to remember

“The Nazi and Soviet regimes turned people into numbers… It is for us as scholars to seek those numbers and to put them into perspective. It is for us as humanists to turn the numbers back into people.”

— Timothy Snyder, closing pages of Bloodlands

What to carry forward: the book’s trick is a change of frame, not of facts. Fix the territory instead of the regime, and two separate national histories collapse into one catastrophe with two authors — and the numbers stop belonging to either of them.
