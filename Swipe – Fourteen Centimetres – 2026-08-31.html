Fourteen Centimetres

That is how little water stood at one gauge on the Rhine this summer — and it was enough to rattle the German economy.

Ones and Tooze (Foreign Policy) · Adam Tooze & Cameron Abadi · 21 Aug 2026 · ~5 min read

Swipe →

5 / 13 →

The hook

A single shallow gauge on the Rhine can knock 0.4% off Germany’s GDP.

When Europe’s busiest inland river runs low, it doesn’t just strand pleasure boats. It throttles the barges that feed Germany’s chemical plants, power stations and factories — and this summer the Rhine fell to its lowest level since records began in 1880.

6 / 13 →

The big idea

Climate change is turning a river into a chokepoint.

On Ones and Tooze, Adam Tooze and Cameron Abadi pick two numbers to explain the world. Here the number is the water level at Kaub — the narrow, shallow point that quietly sets a ceiling on how much cargo can move up the Rhine, and so on a slice of German industry itself.

7 / 13 →
Finding 1 / 5 · The artery

Europe’s busiest waterway

Around 600 vessels a day cross the Dutch–German border on the Rhine, moving on the order of 200 million tonnes of freight a year: oil products, chemicals, coal, iron ore, grain and containers, shuttling between the North Sea ports and the industrial heartland.

8 / 13 →
Finding 2 / 5 · The chokepoint

Kaub sets the ceiling

One shallow narrows near Kaub limits the whole river. As the water drops, deep-loaded barges can’t get through, so they must lighten their cargo — or stop altogether. Traders watch the Kaub gauge the way they watch a stock price.

9 / 13 →
Finding 3 / 5 · The transmission

How low water becomes a GDP hit

It happened in 2018. The Kiel Institute found that low water that autumn cut German industrial production by about 1.5% and shaved roughly 0.4% off GDP; manufacturing output fell around 1% when Kaub stayed below the 78 cm mark for a month. A dry river is a genuine macro shock.

10 / 13 →
Finding 4 / 5 · Who feels it

Chemicals, coal and cooling water

Chemical giants on the Rhine — BASF at Ludwigshafen, Covestro, Evonik — rely on barges to move materials and on the river itself for cooling water. Coal, diesel and grain shift to costlier rail and road, and freight rates spike as one barge’s load has to be split across several.

11 / 13 →
Finding 5 / 5 · The pattern

A preview, not a freak event

Droughts hit in 2018 and 2022; 2026 broke the record. As heatwaves grow more frequent, ‘low water’ stops being a rare accident and becomes a recurring tax on Europe’s industrial core — a textbook case of climate change arriving through the supply chain.

12 / 13 →
Why this matters now

The 2026 record low

A fourth heatwave this summer drove the Rhine to record lows. In early August 2026 the gauge at Kaub fell to about 14 cm — the lowest since measurements began in 1880, undercutting even the 25 cm of 2018. Barges were loading as little as 20–25% of capacity, and forecasters warned it could take weeks of rain to recover, dragging the disruption into October.

The takeaway

A heatwave you can measure in centimetres of missing water can still be felt in a nation’s GDP.

The Rhine is a reminder that climate change often arrives not as a distant abstraction but through the plumbing of trade — one shallow gauge at a time.
