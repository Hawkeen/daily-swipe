No More Fables

Italian Neorealism — when cinema left the studio and walked into the street

Film · Rossellini · De Sica · Visconti · Zavattini · c.1945–1952 · ~5 min read

Swipe →

5 / 15 →

The hook

No stars. No studio. No script worth the name. Just a father, a son, and a stolen bicycle — and a new idea of what a film could be.

Out of the devastation of the Second World War and the fall of Fascism, a handful of Italian directors picked up cameras and pointed them at ordinary life. The movement they made would echo through world cinema for decades.

6 / 15 →

The core idea

Show reality, not fables: the everyday struggles of the working class and the poor, filmed where they actually happen.

Italian Neorealism emphasised naturalistic portrayals of ordinary post-war life through on-location shooting, non-professional actors and minimalist production. Its subject was the common person; its style, a deliberate rejection of Hollywood polish.

Finding 1 / 7 · Where it came from

Born in the rubble

The movement originated in Italy in the mid-1940s, amid the devastation of the Second World War and the fall of Fascism. With studios wrecked and money scarce, necessity and conviction pushed film-makers out of the sound stage and into the ruined streets.

8 / 15 →
Finding 2 / 7 · The method

Real streets, real faces

Directors such as Rossellini, De Sica and Visconti shot on real locations with non-professional actors, tiny budgets and a realist aesthetic. Ordinary people played ordinary people, so audiences could recognise their own simple, relatable lives on screen.

9 / 15 →
Finding 3 / 7 · The breakthrough

Rome, Open City (1945)

Roberto Rossellini’s account of life and resistance in Rome under Nazi occupation announced the movement on the world stage — and took the Grand Prize at Cannes in 1946. It proved that a film made from wartime scarcity could carry enormous moral force.

10 / 15 →
Finding 4 / 7 · The masterpiece

Bicycle Thieves (1948)

Widely regarded as the movement’s finest work, Vittorio De Sica’s film follows a man and his son searching Rome for a stolen bicycle he needs to keep his job. De Sica and Cesare Zavattini built the script from little more than the title of a minor novel by the poet Luigi Bartolini.

11 / 15 →
Finding 5 / 7 · The theorist

Zavattini’s manifesto

Screenwriter Cesare Zavattini — De Sica’s lifelong collaborator — gave the movement its creed: a documentary style of realism, non-professional actors, a rejection of Hollywood conventions, real locations over studio sets, an avoidance of dramatic or intrusive editing, and everyday subject matter about the common man.

12 / 15 →
Finding 6 / 7 · The idea of realism

A single unremarkable event

Zavattini’s stories were remarkably simple and direct, concerned with the minutiae of a single unremarkable event in an ordinary person’s life. The drama came not from twists but from attention — the conviction that everyday life, watched closely enough, is enough.

13 / 15 →
Finding 7 / 7 · The reach

A mark on world cinema

Italian Neorealism made its mark on the international stage — Rome, Open City at Cannes, Bicycle Thieves as a touchstone of world cinema. Its insistence on real places, real people and unhurried attention became one of the most influential currents in the history of film.

14 / 15 →

The takeaway

“It would be no exaggeration to say that Ladri di Biciclette is the story of a walk through Rome by a father and his son.”

— André Bazin, What Is Cinema?

Bazin’s line captures the whole revolution: strip away plot machinery and stars, and what remains — a walk, a bond, a city — can hold everything. Neorealism trusted reality to carry the film.
