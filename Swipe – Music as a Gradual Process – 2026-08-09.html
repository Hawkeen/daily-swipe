Music as a Gradual Process

A tape accident became ‘phasing’ — and Steve Reich rebuilt music out of pattern, pulse and audible process

Steve Reich · minimalism & phase music · from It’s Gonna Rain (1965) to Music for 18 Musicians (1976) · ~6 min read

The hook

What if the whole piece of music was just one process, slowly unfolding — and you could hear it happening?

In 1965 two tape loops drifted out of sync in Steve Reich’s hands, and he heard a new kind of music being born. From that accident he built minimalism’s hypnotic patterns and steady pulse.

6 / 15 →

The core idea

Set a simple process running and let it play itself out in the open — the music is the process, heard.

Reich pioneered minimal music by blending classical technique with African drumming, jazz and pop into hypnotic repeating patterns over a steady pulse. His signature move: gradual, perceptible processes that the listener can follow from start to finish.

Finding 1 / 6 · The sound

Pattern, pulse, repetition

A pioneer of minimalism, Reich transformed the 20th-century soundscape with hypnotic repeating patterns and a steady pulse — smashing musical boundaries by mixing classical writing with African drumming, jazz and pop.

Finding 2 / 6 · The accident

Two tapes drift apart

In 1965 Reich made It’s Gonna Rain from a recording of a street preacher in San Francisco’s Union Square. Two identical loops, played on imprecise machines, gradually fell out of sync — and he chose to exploit the drift, exploring every phase relationship until the loops realigned.

Finding 3 / 6 · Phasing goes live

Two pianists, one drifting apart

Reich transferred phasing from tape to live performers in Piano Phase: one pianist repeats a melodic pattern while the other plays the same sequence at a fractionally faster tempo, so the two slide out of — and back into — alignment before your ears.

Finding 4 / 6 · The manifesto

‘Music as a Gradual Process’

In his 1968 essay Reich set out the aesthetic: he wanted processes you could hear unfolding. Once a process is set up and loaded, he wrote, it ‘runs by itself’ — the composer starts it, then steps back and listens with everyone else.

Finding 5 / 6 · The masterwork

Music for 18 Musicians

Developed in rehearsal between 1974 and 1976, Music for 18 Musicians is organised in eleven sections, each built on a single chord. On release it made a massive impact, inspiring generations across experimental music, rock and contemporary electronic music.

Finding 6 / 6 · Beyond phasing

Trains, speech and a Pulitzer

With Different Trains (1988) Reich turned recorded speech into melody, drawing on his Jewish heritage and history; it won a Grammy for Best Contemporary Composition in 1990 with the Kronos Quartet. In 2009 he won the Pulitzer Prize in Music for Double Sextet.

Why this matters in 2026

Reich at 90, and a masterwork at 50

2026 marks Steve Reich’s 90th birthday and the 50th anniversary of Music for 18 Musicians. From 14–18 October the Park Avenue Armory stages the work in its vast Drill Hall, and festivals such as Prague Sounds mount ‘Steve Reich: 90’ celebrations worldwide.

The takeaway

“I am interested in perceptible processes. I want to be able to hear the process happening throughout the sounding music.”

— Steve Reich, Music as a Gradual Process (1968). Start something simple, let it run in the open, and listening itself becomes the drama.
