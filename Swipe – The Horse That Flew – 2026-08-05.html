The Horse That Flew

How a bet about a galloping horse froze time — and set cinema in motion

Eadweard Muybridge · The Horse in Motion · Palo Alto, 19 June 1878 · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 15 →

The hook

When a horse gallops, is there a moment when all four hooves leave the ground at once?

For centuries no eye was fast enough to say. In 1878, on a track in Palo Alto, a photographer built a machine to answer the question — and in doing so, learned to stop time.

6 / 15 →

The idea

Break a movement into slices thin enough, and you can see what motion hides — then run the slices back and motion returns.

Eadweard Muybridge’s sequence photography did both. It dissected the gallop into frozen instants no eye could catch, and it pointed straight at the projected moving image — the birth of cinema.

Finding 1 / 6 · The photographer

From Yosemite to obsession

Born Edward Muggeridge in Kingston upon Thames in 1830, he sailed to America, reinvented himself as ‘Eadweard Muybridge’, and won fame in the late 1860s for sweeping photographs of the Yosemite Valley — before turning his lens on the problem of movement.

Finding 2 / 6 · The commission

A railroad baron’s hunch

In 1872 Leland Stanford — former California governor and railroad magnate — hired Muybridge to photograph a horse at full gallop. Stanford suspected there was an instant of ‘unsupported transit’, when no hoof touched the earth. The naked eye could not confirm it.

Finding 3 / 6 · The machine

Twelve cameras and a tripwire

On 19 June 1878, the mare Sallie Gardner ran past a battery of twelve cameras. As she passed, she tripped threads that released each shutter in about 1/1000th of a second. The frozen frames proved it: at full stretch, all four hooves do leave the ground at once.

Finding 4 / 6 · The scandal

A murder in the middle of the story

The work was interrupted in 1874 when Muybridge shot and killed his wife’s lover. He was tried and acquitted — the jury accepting a plea of justifiable homicide. Only after the trial did he return to Stanford’s horses and resume the experiments.

Finding 5 / 6 · The projector

The zoopraxiscope

In 1879 Muybridge built the zoopraxiscope, a device that projected images from a spinning glass disc so they appeared to move. Predating the flexible film strip, it is counted among the first movie projectors — the missing link between the still photograph and cinema.

Finding 6 / 6 · The atlas of motion

20,000 images of moving things

At the University of Pennsylvania in 1884–85 he made Animal Locomotion: 781 collotype plates, each holding up to 36 frames — over 20,000 images of humans and animals walking, running, leaping. It became a vast visual dictionary of movement for artists and scientists alike.

Why this matters in 2026

The origin of the moving image, revisited

Muybridge is having a moment: Fujifilm Square in Tokyo mounted Animal Locomotion as the Origin of Moving Images, framing him as ‘the man obsessed with sequence photography’. In an age of frame-by-frame video, motion capture and machine vision, the man who first sliced movement into frames feels newly close.

14 / 15 →

The takeaway

A bet about a horse became the moment photography learned to stop time — and, by running the frozen frames back to life, taught pictures to move.

Muybridge sits at a hinge in the history of images: at once a scientist proving a fact the eye can’t see, an artist making a dictionary of motion, and the accidental grandfather of cinema.
