Episode of the day · 🎧

The Rebellion That Broke the Tang

A trusted frontier general turned his army on the emperor — and China’s most brilliant age never fully recovered.

In Our Time · BBC Radio 4 · 16 Feb 2012 · ~42 min
Melvyn Bragg with Frances Wood, Naomi Standen & Hilde de Weerdt

Swipe →

The hook

How does a golden age end in eight short years?

The Tang dynasty was the dazzling high point of imperial China — cosmopolitan, confident, rich. Then, in 755, a favoured general named An Lushan marched on the capitals. The war that followed lasted until 763 and left the empire hollowed out.

6 / 12 →

The core of it

The An Lushan Rebellion was the great turning point of medieval China — the moment the Tang broke.

It began as a commandery revolt aiming to topple the Tang and install a rival ‘Yan’ dynasty. It failed to replace the Tang — but it permanently weakened it, and shifted power away from the imperial centre for good.

Finding 1 / 5 · The man

Who was An Lushan?

A general of non-Chinese descent — his father a Sogdian from Central Asia, his mother of Turkish origin. He rose through the Tang army in the 740s to become a powerful military governor and a personal favourite of Emperor Xuanzong.

8 / 12 →
Finding 2 / 5 · The revolt

Two capitals fall

Driven by ambition and a bitter rivalry with the powerful Yang family at court, An Lushan turned his troops on the eastern capital, Luoyang, in 755, took it and proclaimed himself emperor. Six months later his forces seized the western capital, Chang’an.

9 / 12 →
Finding 3 / 5 · The flight

The emperor flees to Shu

Attrib. Li Zhaodao, ‘Emperor Minghuang’s Journey to Shu’. Public domain via Wikimedia Commons.

In summer 756 Emperor Xuanzong fled south-west to Shu (modern Sichuan). An Lushan himself was murdered in 757 — but the war ground on, and was not finally put down until 763.

10 / 12 →
Finding 4 / 5 · The toll

A demographic abyss

The Tang census recorded a population fall from about 53 million in 754 to 17 million in 764. Much of that reflects not only battle deaths but famine, mass displacement and the collapse of the tax-and-census apparatus — still, the scale of dislocation was staggering.

11 / 12 →
Finding 5 / 5 · The aftermath

Power leaves the centre

Crushing the revolt empowered the very forces that had caused it: the jiedushi, provincial military governors, grew mighty at the expense of the scholar-officials. The Tang lost the Western Regions permanently — and slid, over the next century, toward fragmentation.

12 / 12 ✓

The takeaway

“The nation is broken, though mountains and rivers remain.”

— Du Fu, ‘Spring View’, written in 757 while the poet was trapped in rebel-held Chang’an. The An Lushan Rebellion failed to seize the throne for good — yet it broke the Tang and remade the map of Chinese power.
