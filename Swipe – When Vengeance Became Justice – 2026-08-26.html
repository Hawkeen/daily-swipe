When Vengeance Became Justice

Theatre · Aeschylus’s Oresteia (458 BCE): how the stage turned blood-feud into the rule of law.

Aeschylus · Oresteia · first staged 458 BCE · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 14 →

The hook

How do you stop a cycle of revenge that keeps killing, generation after generation?

That is the question at the heart of the Oresteia — the only complete tragic trilogy to survive from ancient Greece. Its answer would help invent an idea we still live by: the court of law.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

Aeschylus dramatises the passage from private blood-vengeance to public justice — and stages the birth of the jury court.

Across three plays, an endless cycle of murder within one cursed family is finally broken not by more killing, but by a trial. The goddess Athena founds Athens’s first homicide court and persuades the spirits of vengeance to accept the rule of law.

Finding 1 / 5 · The curse

A family that cannot stop killing

The trilogy — Agamemnon, The Libation Bearers, The Eumenides — follows the doomed House of Atreus. Agamemnon returns from Troy and is murdered by his wife Clytemnestra (avenging their sacrificed daughter); their son Orestes then kills Clytemnestra, on Apollo’s command.

8 / 14 →
Finding 2 / 5 · The dilemma

Right against right

This is not good versus evil but a clash of conflicting justices — ‘right with right’. Each avenger is, in their own terms, justified. That is precisely why vengeance can never end: every act of justice creates the next victim owed revenge.

9 / 14 →
Finding 3 / 5 · The verdict

Athena founds the first court

The Furies hunt Orestes for spilling a mother’s blood. Athena intervenes — she establishes the Areopagus, Athens’s first homicide court, and a jury of citizens weighs the case. Reasoned civic judgement replaces the blood-feud. The Furies are transformed into protective spirits of the city.

10 / 14 →
Finding 4 / 5 · The politics

A myth for a city in flux

The Oresteia landed in a charged moment: only a few years after the assassination of the reformer Ephialtes (462 BCE), as democratic reforms were stripping the old Areopagus of power. Aeschylus retells the court’s divine origin just as Athens argues over what it should be.

11 / 14 →
Finding 5 / 5 · The craft

The invention of dramatic conflict

Aeschylus, often called the father of tragedy, was a technical pioneer: by adding a second actor, he made real dialogue and confrontation possible on stage. Drama could now show characters in conflict — not just a single voice answering a chorus.

Why this matters in 2026

The Oresteia, on stage in 2026

The trilogy is live theatre right now. A major new Oresteia, written and directed by Simon Stone, opened at London’s Bridge Theatre on 14 July 2026 (previews from 2 July), starring Mary-Louise Parker and David Morrissey. Set on a rotating two-storey house, it drops a contemporary family into the myth — and runs until 19 September 2026.

The takeaway

“Wisdom comes by suffering.”

— Aeschylus, Agamemnon (trans. Herbert Weir Smyth). The Greek phrase páthei máthos — ‘learning through suffering’ — is the moral engine of the whole trilogy.
