China’s Two Economies

A world-beating tech machine bolted to a domestic economy that keeps stalling

Pekingology (CSIS) · ‘Inside China’s Dual Economy’ · Henrietta Levin with Alicia García-Herrero · 6 Aug 2026 · ~5 min read

Swipe →

5 / 12 →

The hook

China’s companies dominate whole industries — and still can’t turn a profit. Why does Beijing keep pushing?

Economist Alicia García-Herrero joins Henrietta Levin to unpick a paradox at the heart of the world’s second-largest economy: soaring technological ambition running alongside thin margins and a sluggish home market.

6 / 12 →

The core argument

Xi has chosen global technological leadership over economic opportunity at home — and China now runs, in effect, two economies at once.

One is a frontier tech-and-export machine; the other a domestic economy that lags behind. The open question, García-Herrero argues, is whether rapid AI adoption will finally lift growth — or strain social stability.

7 / 12 →
Finding 1 / 4 · The split

One country, two economies

The episode’s framing is a dual economy: a world-leading advanced-technology and export sector sitting atop a domestic, consumer-facing economy that trails behind. The two do not rise together — and the gap is a policy choice, not an accident.

8 / 12 →
Finding 2 / 4 · The priority

Tech leadership over prosperity

García-Herrero’s central claim: President Xi has prioritized global technological leadership over economic opportunity at home. Strategic dominance in frontier industries ranks above near-term household welfare and consumption.

9 / 12 →
Finding 3 / 4 · The puzzle

Winning share, losing money

Here’s the twist that unsettles the ‘China unstoppable’ story: many of China’s most successful companies struggle to turn a profit. Market dominance and financial health, in this model, are not the same thing.

10 / 12 →
Finding 4 / 4 · The gamble

AI: engine or fault line?

The wager on which the model rests: whether rapid AI adoption will boost growth or imperil social stability. The same automation that could rescue productivity might also disrupt jobs and livelihoods — a political risk, not just an economic one.

11 / 12 →

The takeaway

China’s tech dominance and its household prosperity are pulling in different directions — by design.

Read through García-Herrero’s dual-economy lens, Beijing is spending near-term profits and consumption to buy long-term technological supremacy — and betting that AI can square the circle before social strains bite.
