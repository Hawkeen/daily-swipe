Creative topic · 🎨 Architecture

Designed by Gravity

To design a cathedral, Antoni Gaudí didn’t reach for a ruler. He hung chains from the ceiling — and let gravity draw the curves.

Antoni Gaudí · La Sagrada Família · Barcelona · begun 1882 · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 14 →

The hook

How do you design a cathedral? Gaudí hung it upside-down.

To find the perfect shape for his arches and towers, Gaudí suspended chains and little weighted bags from a board on the ceiling. Gravity pulled them into elegant curves — and when he turned the whole model upside-down, those curves became the strongest possible arches in stone.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

Let nature and gravity do the engineering.

Gaudí was convinced that the forms and forces of nature — trees, bones, hanging ropes — had already solved the problems architects struggle with. The Sagrada Família translates those natural forms into stone, trading the straight line and the right angle for the curve.

Idea 1 / 6 · A lifetime

One man, forty years, unfinished

Construction began in 1882; Gaudí took over in 1883 and gave the rest of his life to it — the last dozen years almost exclusively. He died in 1926, struck by a tram, with the church far from finished.

8 / 14 →
Idea 2 / 6 · Nature

The curve is truer than the line

Gaudí studied trees, bones, shells and honeycombs and drew his geometry straight from them. Where classical architecture prized the straight line and the right angle, he insisted the curve was closer to the way the world is actually built — and closer to the divine.

9 / 14 →
Idea 3 / 6 · The chain

Tension becomes compression

His signature tool was the hanging (funicular) model. A chain slung between two points falls into a catenary, in pure tension. Flip that shape upside-down and it becomes an arch in pure compression — every stone squeezed, never bent. Gaudí designed by letting gravity find the strongest line for him.

10 / 14 →
Idea 4 / 6 · Four shapes

A whole church from four surfaces

Almost the entire building is composed from four geometric surfaces — the hyperboloid, hyperbolic paraboloid, helicoid and conoid. Each is a curved surface generated by straight lines, and each appears in nature: in bones, tree-trunks and leaves. Complex beauty, built from a tiny vocabulary.

11 / 14 →
Idea 5 / 6 · A stone forest

Columns that branch like trees

Because the geometry channels the loads straight down, Gaudí’s branching, tree-like columns hold up the vaults without the flying buttresses Gothic cathedrals relied on — supports he reportedly dismissed as ‘crutches’. Hyperboloid openings scatter daylight through the nave like sun through a canopy.

Idea 6 / 6 · Built on alms

An expiatory temple

The Sagrada Família is a Temple Expiatori — funded entirely by donations and alms, not the state. Gaudí even left his own estate to the works. That reliance on gifts is a large part of why a single church has taken well over a century to build.

Why this matters in 2026

After 144 years, the top is on

On 20 February 2026, workers fixed the upper arm of the cross onto the central Tower of Jesus Christ — the last and tallest of the basilica’s 18 towers — bringing it to about 172.5 m and making the Sagrada Família the tallest church in the world. On 10 June 2026, the centenary of Gaudí’s death, Pope Leo XIV was due to bless it. Interior work continues into 2027–2028.

The takeaway

“The straight line belongs to men, the curved line belongs to God.”

— attributed to Antoni Gaudí. A century after his death, the church he designed by hanging chains and copying trees has topped out at last — proof that the slowest way to build can also be the most radical.
