Book of the day · 📖

Where Chaos Begins

A butterfly flaps its wings, and the forecast falls apart

James Gleick · Chaos: Making a New Science · 1987 · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 14 →

The hook

Why can we land a probe on a comet, yet not tell you the weather three weeks from Tuesday?

The same tidy equations that describe a swinging pendulum can, in other systems, behave wildly. Gleick’s bestseller tells how a scattered band of scientists took disorder seriously — and found a hidden order inside it.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

Beneath apparent randomness — turbulence, weather, wildlife booms, the beating heart — lies structure.

Chaos is not the absence of law but the surprising behaviour of simple, fully deterministic rules. A system can obey exact equations and still be, for all practical purposes, unpredictable.

Finding 1 / 6 · The accident

A rounding error that broke the weather

Image: Wikimol / Dschwen, CC BY-SA, via Wikimedia Commons.

In 1961 meteorologist Edward Lorenz re-ran a weather simulation, typing 0.506 in place of the stored 0.506127. That one-part-in-a-thousand change produced a completely different two months of weather — the first glimpse of “sensitive dependence on initial conditions”.

Finding 2 / 6 · The butterfly

A butterfly’s wing in Brazil

At a 1972 meeting Lorenz gave a talk titled “Predictability: Does the flap of a butterfly’s wings in Brazil set off a tornado in Texas?” The metaphor stuck: a tiny, seemingly trivial perturbation can reshape the whole. Forecasting has a horizon we cannot cross.

Finding 3 / 6 · The geometry

Coastlines, clouds and self-similarity

Image: Wolfgang Beyer, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Benoit Mandelbrot showed that coastlines, clouds and price charts are “self-similar” — the same rough shape recurring at every scale. His fractals gave the new science of chaos a geometry to see with.

10 / 14 →
Finding 4 / 6 · The surprise

Universality: the same road to chaos

Mitchell Feigenbaum discovered that utterly different systems slide from order into chaos by the same route — a cascade of “period-doublings” governed by the same universal constant. Order, improbably, hides inside the disorder.

11 / 14 →
Finding 5 / 6 · The reach

A dripping tap, a fibrillating heart

Gleick gathers dripping taps, fluttering hearts, animal populations, turbulence and pendulums. Chaos crossed disciplines to become a science of its own, threaded by four ideas: sensitive dependence, self-similarity, universality and nonlinearity.

12 / 14 →
Finding 6 / 6 · The twist

Determined, yet unpredictable

The deepest shock is philosophical. A system can be perfectly deterministic — no randomness in its rules — and still defy long-term prediction. The wall is not our ignorance; it is written into the mathematics itself.

13 / 14 →

The takeaway

“Where chaos begins, classical science stops.”

— James Gleick, Chaos. For three centuries science prized the smooth and the predictable; Gleick’s generation found that the irregular and the unpredictable were where the deepest new questions lay.
