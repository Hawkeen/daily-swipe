The Robots Are Here

Why humanoid robots are the real general-purpose technology — and how Unitree took pole position

ChinaTalk · with SemiAnalysis’s Niko Ciminelli & Reyk Knuhtsen · 4 July 2026 · ~5 min read

Swipe →

5 / 15 →

The hook

The next general-purpose technology may not be a chatbot. It may be a body — and the cheapest ones are being built in China.

SemiAnalysis’s robotics team makes the case that humanoid robots are arriving faster than most expect, and that the same playbook the West used to dismiss DJI and BYD is causing it to underestimate Unitree.

6 / 15 →

The core idea

China’s edge in humanoid robots is not a breakthrough algorithm — it is vertical integration and a supply chain that makes robots dramatically cheaper.

Vertical integration, actuator manufacturing and deep supply chains make Chinese humanoids much cheaper than American ones — and cheap, reliable hardware is what lets a general-purpose technology actually get deployed at scale.

7 / 15 →
Finding 1 / 6 · Why robots matter

The real general-purpose technology

The guests argue robots — not any single software model — are the general-purpose technology of this cycle: a machine that can, in principle, be pointed at almost any physical task. That breadth is what makes the manufacturing race for them so consequential.

8 / 15 →
Finding 2 / 6 · The blind spot

Underestimating Unitree, again

Everyone keeps underestimating Unitree through the DJI and BYD playbooks — assuming a Chinese hardware upstart can’t be serious until it already dominates. On this reading, the same mistake is being repeated with humanoid robots.

9 / 15 →
Finding 3 / 6 · Where the cost lives

Actuators are the whole game

Joint actuators — the motors that move a robot’s limbs — are the single largest component cost in a humanoid. Win on actuators and you win on price; and price is what decides whether robots stay in labs or spread across factories.

10 / 15 →
Finding 4 / 6 · The EV crossover

Down an order of magnitude in three years

Chinese supply chains have driven the cost of high-performance actuators down by roughly an order of magnitude over three years — partly because the same suppliers that serve China’s EV makers can serve its robot makers. Electric cars quietly built the parts bin for humanoids.

11 / 15 →
Finding 5 / 6 · The Unitree method

Make your own actuators, then iterate

By manufacturing its own actuators at scale, Unitree has driven that cost down roughly 50% versus sourcing outside. Fast iteration cycles — bigger actuators, added cooling, better designs — made its robots more performant at lower cost. That combination is why it has done so well.

12 / 15 →
Finding 6 / 6 · The pattern

The BYD playbook, applied to bodies

The strategy mirrors BYD in electric vehicles: own the key components, iterate relentlessly, and let a vast domestic supply chain compress cost. Applied to humanoids, it turns a research curiosity into a manufacturable, exportable product.

13 / 15 →
Why this matters in 2026

IPO in Shanghai, ban in Washington

In early July 2026 Unitree passed its STAR Market IPO registration, raising over 4.2 billion yuan, with a full-year shipment target of 20,000 units. Days later, on 28 July 2026, the US FCC moved to ban Chinese humanoid robots and power inverters it linked to confirmed backdoors — the security backlash arriving in step with the technology.

The takeaway

“Robots are here, and they’re going to change the world, with Unitree currently in pole position.”

— ChinaTalk, The Robots Are Here

The lesson echoes the EV story: the country that owns the boring, physical middle of the supply chain — the actuators, the assembly, the iteration — may end up owning the robots too.
