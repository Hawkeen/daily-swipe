Africa’s Industrial Behemoth

Nigeria pumps crude, then buys back petrol at a premium. One private refinery is trying to break that trap.

Odd Lots (Bloomberg) · 24 Aug 2026 · with Joe Studwell · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 14 →

The hook

Why would Africa’s biggest oil producer spend decades importing the fuel it drives on?

For years Nigeria exported crude and re-imported refined petrol at a markup, while its state refineries sat idle. Then its richest man built the biggest refinery on the continent — and turned a national embarrassment into a test case for African industry.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

The Dangote refinery is a wager on whether Africa can finally industrialise — and a live test of the Asian development playbook.

Odd Lots hosts Joe Studwell, author of How Africa Works, who argues that most African economies have not adopted the export-driven industrial policy that lifted East Asia. The refinery is where that debate meets reality.

7 / 14 →
Finding 1 / 5 · The paradox

$18 billion, zero working refineries

Nigeria has three state-owned refineries. The government spent an estimated $18 billion maintaining them over two decades — yet they don’t work, forcing the country to export crude and import refined products at a premium.

8 / 14 →
Finding 2 / 5 · The builder

What the state couldn’t, a tycoon did

Aliko Dangote — Africa’s richest person, with a fortune reported between roughly $28 and $37 billion — built the first economically successful refinery in Lagos. It aims to meet all of Nigeria’s fuel demand using about 60% of its capacity, and export the rest.

9 / 14 →
Finding 3 / 5 · The policy

Import substitution, made concrete

This is textbook import substitution. A new 15% import duty on refined fuel tilts the field toward domestic refining — a decisive policy shift toward self-sufficiency, and exactly the kind of state-shaped market Studwell studies.

10 / 14 →
Finding 4 / 5 · The lens

Why Asia industrialised and Africa mostly hasn’t

Studwell’s thesis: countries get rich by forcing firms to compete in export markets and steering finance to industry. Africa, he argues, has largely skipped that discipline — which is why one refinery can’t, by itself, settle the continent’s industrial question.

11 / 14 →
Finding 5 / 5 · The catch

The obstacles that don’t vanish

Even a triumph reveals the constraints: the Dangote saga has been marked by regulatory uncertainty, infrastructure deficits and persistent import dependence — the same structural features that have long held back industrialisation across much of Africa.

12 / 14 →
Why this matters in 2026

Africa’s largest IPO is coming

The refinery is heading for the public markets. Dangote is targeting a listing on the Nigerian Exchange around September–October 2026, with about $1 billion in underwriting secured and reports of a raise that could reach ~$5 billion — on track to be the largest IPO in African history.

13 / 14 →

The takeaway

“Success and Failure on the World’s Last Developmental Frontier”

— the subtitle of Joe Studwell’s How Africa Works. The Dangote refinery is one bet on which side of that line Africa lands.
