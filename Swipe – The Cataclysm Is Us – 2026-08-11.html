Book of the day · 📖

The Cataclysm Is Us

Five times, life on Earth has almost been erased. This time the asteroid has a name — us

Elizabeth Kolbert · The Sixth Extinction: An Unnatural History · 2014 · Pulitzer Prize 2015 · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 15 →

The hook

What if the most devastating extinction since the dinosaurs is happening right now — caused by a single species?

Kolbert’s reporting gathers the evidence that human activity is driving the loss of as many as half of all living species — a sixth mass extinction. Her subtitle is the sting: this is an unnatural history.

6 / 15 →

The core idea

Life on Earth has been nearly wiped out five times by natural catastrophe. ‘This time around the cataclysm is us.’

Scientists across half a dozen disciplines are now watching a sixth extinction unfold in real time — and its driver is not a volcano or an asteroid but human beings, altering the atmosphere, oceans and land faster than life can adapt.

7 / 15 →
Finding 1 / 6 · The Big Five

Deep time’s five great die-offs

Over the last half-billion years there have been five mass extinctions, when the diversity of life suddenly and dramatically contracted. The sixth, scientists predict, could be the most devastating since the asteroid impact that wiped out the dinosaurs.

8 / 15 →
Finding 2 / 6 · The driver

A mass extinction with a culprit

The previous five were set off by cosmic and geological accident. This one has an agent: us. Kolbert lays out the evidence that human activity is now the force contracting the diversity of life — potentially the loss of up to half of all living species.

9 / 15 →
Finding 3 / 6 · The sea

Running geology at warp speed

Oceans absorb about a third of the carbon dioxide we emit. The danger, Kolbert argues, isn’t the CO₂ itself but the speed — we are ‘running geologic history … at warp speed’. Ocean acidification played a role in at least two of the Big Five (the end-Permian and end-Triassic).

10 / 15 →
Finding 4 / 6 · The witnesses

A dozen species, told in the field

John James Audubon, ‘Great Auk’, The Birds of America (Plate 341, 1836). Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Kolbert travels with geologists reading ocean cores, botanists chasing the tree line up the Andes, and divers on the Great Barrier Reef. She anchors the science in a dozen creatures — the Panamanian golden frog, staghorn coral, the great auk, the Sumatran rhino.

11 / 15 →
Finding 5 / 6 · The tempo

Too fast to adapt

The thread running through every chapter is pace. The rate at which humans are changing the oceans and other environments is unprecedented in geological history — leaving life forms almost no time to adapt naturally without a major die-off.

12 / 15 →
Finding 6 / 6 · The legacy

Our most lasting mark

The sixth extinction, Kolbert observes, is likely to be humankind’s most enduring legacy — outlasting our art, our cities, everything. It compels us to rethink the most basic question of what it means to be human.

13 / 15 →
Why this matters in 2026

The ledger keeps growing

The IUCN Red List update of 9 July 2026 now assesses 169,420 species, of which 47,187 are threatened with extinction, and it declared 15 more animals extinct. Nearly two-thirds of assessed deep-sea hydrothermal-vent molluscs are threatened, and more than 42% of Europe’s freshwater fishes — the drum-beat of Kolbert’s book, in this year’s data.

14 / 15 →

The takeaway

“Right now, in the amazing moment that to us counts as the present, we are deciding, without quite meaning to, which evolutionary pathways will remain open and which will forever be closed.”

— Elizabeth Kolbert. No other creature has ever managed this, she adds — and it will, unfortunately, be our most enduring legacy.
