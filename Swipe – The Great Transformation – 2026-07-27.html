Book of the day · 📖

The Great Transformation

The Political and Economic Origins of Our Time

Karl Polanyi · Farrar & Rinehart · 1944 · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 14 →

The hook

The “free market” did not arise naturally. It had to be planned into existence — and it nearly tore society apart.

Writing in wartime exile, Polanyi set out to explain how the liberal, market-driven civilisation of the nineteenth century had collapsed into depression, fascism and world war within a single lifetime. His answer inverts the usual story about capitalism’s rise.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

A society run entirely by a self-regulating market cannot sustain itself — so society always fights back.

Markets, Polanyi argued, had always been embedded in social relations. The nineteenth century tried to reverse that — to subordinate society to the logic of the market. Because that project threatened people and nature, it triggered protective counter-movements. The clash between the two is the hidden engine of modern history.

Finding 1 / 6 · The trick at the heart of it

Land, labour and money are “fictitious commodities”

A market economy treats everything as if it were produced for sale. But land is nature, labour is human beings, and money is a token of purchasing power — none of them was actually made to be sold. Polanyi calls them fictitious commodities: things pre-dating the market that were forced to behave as commodities.

8 / 14 →
Finding 2 / 6 · Laissez-faire was planned

The market was built by the state

The self-regulating market did not emerge spontaneously from human nature. Turning land, labour and money into tradable commodities was part of deliberate, state-led transformations designed to create a market society. In one of Polanyi’s sharpest reversals: planning was needed to impose “free” markets.

9 / 14 →
Finding 3 / 6 · Disembedding

The economy torn loose from society

Laissez-faire reformers sought to “disembed” the economy — to build a market society in which all things, including the fictitious commodities, are commodified. For Polanyi this is a utopia: a self-regulating market cannot itself be the basis of social order, because a society governed purely by supply and demand would consume the very people and land it depends on.

10 / 14 →
Finding 4 / 6 · The key mechanism

The double movement

History since the Industrial Revolution runs on two opposing dynamics. One pushes to extend and free the market; the other pushes society to protect itself — through labour laws, welfare, tariffs, central banking. Polanyi called this the double movement: “a single historical cycle” of market devastation followed by counter-movement.

11 / 14 →
Finding 5 / 6 · The image

The “satanic mill”

Polanyi’s verdict on the unregulated market: “No society could stand the effects of such a system of crude fictions even for the shortest stretch of time unless its human and natural substance as well as its business organisation was protected against the ravages of this satanic mill.” Protection is not an interference with the market — it is what makes society survivable.

12 / 14 →
Finding 6 / 6 · The dark payoff

Fascism as the second transformation

Polanyi devoted the book’s final part to catastrophe. The rise of fascism, he argued, was a result of the liberal utopia of the self-regulating market: when the market system broke down in the 1930s, societies lurched for order. The second “great transformation” — fascism — grew out of the first: the rise of market liberalism.

13 / 14 →

The takeaway

“To allow the market mechanism to be sole director of the fate of human beings and their natural environment… would result in the demolition of society.”

— Karl Polanyi, The Great Transformation

Polanyi’s lesson is not that markets are bad, but that markets left to run society will provoke a reaction — benign or monstrous. Every debate about deregulation, welfare and backlash is, in some sense, a re-run of his double movement.
