Book of the day · 📖

The Signal in the Noise

Ordinary people, using a few simple habits, out-predicted intelligence analysts who could read classified cables. How?

Philip Tetlock & Dan Gardner · Superforecasting · 2015 · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 14 →

The hook

Is forecasting the future a gift — or a skill you can learn?

In a US-government tournament, a self-selected crowd of amateurs beat professional intelligence analysts who had access to secret data. Tetlock’s answer: the best forecasters aren’t smarter or better-informed. They just think differently — and anyone can copy how.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

Good judgement is a measurable, teachable set of habits — not a rare talent or luck.

Drawing on the Good Judgment Project — run by Tetlock, Barbara Mellers and Don Moore at the University of Pennsylvania — the book shows that keeping score, breaking questions apart, and constantly updating beliefs reliably produces sharper predictions.

7 / 14 →
Finding 1 / 7 · The tournament

Amateurs who beat the spies

In the IARPA-sponsored forecasting tournament (2011–2015), a group of top volunteers — the ‘superforecasters’ — outperformed regular forecasters by about 60%, and did roughly 30% better than intelligence-community analysts who could read classified intercepts.

8 / 14 →
Finding 2 / 7 · Fox vs hedgehog

Be a fox, not a hedgehog

Hedgehogs view the world through one big idea, make bold yes/no calls, and resist changing their minds. Foxes gather many viewpoints, express probabilities precisely, and revise often. Across 20 years of geopolitical forecasting, foxes consistently win.

9 / 14 →
Finding 3 / 7 · Keep score

Speak in numbers, then grade yourself

Superforecasters replace vague words like ‘maybe’ with precise probabilities, and score themselves with the Brier score (0 to 1, lower is better), which rewards being well-calibrated: if you say 70%, it should happen about 70% of the time. Only by measuring can you improve.

10 / 14 →
Finding 4 / 7 · Fermi-ize

Break the impossible into pieces

A daunting question becomes tractable when it is ‘Fermi-ized’ — broken into sub-questions you can actually reason about. Start with the outside view (the base rate) before adjusting for the specifics. This tames overconfidence and enables finer-grained estimates.

11 / 14 →
Finding 5 / 7 · Dragonfly eye

See through many eyes at once

Tetlock calls it the ‘dragonfly eye’: deliberately gathering opposing viewpoints and synthesising them, rather than locking onto one story. Paired with actively open-minded thinking — treating beliefs as hypotheses to test — it keeps a forecaster honest.

12 / 14 →
Finding 6 / 7 · Perpetual beta

Always a work in progress

The single strongest predictor of a superforecaster, Tetlock found, is being in ‘perpetual beta’ — committed to updating and self-improvement. Superforecasters revise their predictions two to three times as often as average forecasters, in small, careful steps.

13 / 14 →
Finding 7 / 7 · It can be taught

A skill, and a team sport

The habits above were experimentally shown to boost accuracy — and working in teams, with a readiness to admit error, improved forecasts further. Tetlock’s ‘Ten Commandments’ distil the method, while warning: don’t treat them as rigid rules in a world where nothing is exactly repeatable.

14 / 14 ✓

The takeaway

“For superforecasters, beliefs are hypotheses to be tested, not treasures to be guarded.”

— Philip Tetlock, Superforecasting. Hold your views lightly, keep score, break problems down, and update — and your view of the future gets measurably sharper.
