Two Thousand Miles to Twenty

How two empires spent eighty years closing the gap between them — and called it a game.

Peter Hopkirk · The Great Game: On Secret Service in High Asia
John Murray, 1990 (US title: The Struggle for Empire in Central Asia) · ~6 min read

5 / 15 →

When play began early in the 19th century, the frontiers of the two empires lay two thousand miles apart.

By the end, only twenty miles separated them.

6 / 15 →
The core argument

A rivalry fought by surveyors, envoys and spies

Hopkirk tells the story of the 19th-century contest between the Russian and British empires for supremacy in Central Asia. At stake was the preservation of India, key to the wealth of the British Empire.

Britain aimed to create a protectorate in Afghanistan and to prop up the Ottoman Empire, Persia, Khiva and Bukhara as buffer states — blocking Russia from a port on the Persian Gulf or the Indian Ocean. As the two spheres expanded and collided, Russia proposed Afghanistan as the neutral zone.

Finding 1 / 7

Where the phrase came from

The term was coined in 1840 by Captain Arthur Conolly (1807–1842), a British intelligence officer serving as a political officer. In July 1840 he wrote to Major Henry Rawlinson, newly appointed political agent in Kandahar: “You’ve a great game, a noble game, before you.”

Conolly meant it idealistically — he believed the post was a chance to advance humanitarianism in Afghanistan. The Russian diplomat Karl Nesselrode reportedly used a darker phrase for the same contest: the “Tournament of Shadows”. Kipling’s 1901 novel Kim popularised Conolly’s version, and it surged again after the Soviet–Afghan War began in 1979.

Finding 2 / 7

The starting gun, 12 January 1830

On one major view the Great Game began on 12 January 1830, when Lord Ellenborough, president of the Board of Control for India, tasked the governor-general Lord Bentinck with establishing a trade route to the Emirate of Bukhara.

The logic was pre-emptive rather than acquisitive: protect India and the sea trade routes by denying Russia a warm-water outlet. Everything that followed — the surveys, the envoys, the subsidies to amirs — flowed from that instruction.

Finding 3 / 7

Spies with theodolites

The Game’s first instrument was the survey. In 1831 Captain Alexander Burnes and Colonel Henry Pottinger mapped the Indus; Burnes then made a dangerous twelve-month journey through Afghanistan and the Hindu Kush to Bukhara, returning in 1832. His book Travels to Bukhara was an overnight success in 1834.

In 1835 Lieutenant John Wood took the first steamboat up the Indus, and in 1838 led the expedition that found one of the sources of the Oxus. Meanwhile, in 1837, the Russian envoy Captain Jan Vitkevitch arrived in Kabul — and dined with the British envoy Burnes.

Finding 4 / 7

The catastrophe the Game produced

The India Board had told Lord Auckland to “counteract the progress of Russian influence” in Afghanistan, and to interfere decidedly if he judged the moment had come. He did. The First Anglo-Afghan War followed.

Elizabeth Butler’s painting fixed the result in the British imagination: William Brydon arriving at the gates of Jalalabad as the sole survivor of an evacuation from Kabul, 16,500 strong, in January 1842. Conolly himself was beheaded at Bokhara that same year, accused of spying.

Finding 5 / 7

Russia’s advance, by the calendar

After the Crimean War ended in 1856 with Russia’s defeat, Alexander II waited a few years, then moved in two campaigns. The first ran from Orenburg: Chimkent in 1864, Tashkent in 1865, Khokand and Bukhara in 1866, Samarkand in 1868. The second ran from the Caspian: Krasnovodsk in 1869, Khiva in 1873.

In 1864 the Russian chancellor Gorchakov circulated a note to consular officers abroad explaining the expansion in terms of necessity, power and the spread of civilisation — at pains to insist Russia meant only to bring civilised behaviour and protect trade routes.

Finding 6 / 7

Panjdeh, 1885: the lesson an amir learned

Russian forces took Geok Tepe in 1881 and occupied Merv in 1884. With Russian troops near Herat, London and St Petersburg formed a joint Afghan Boundary Commission — and in 1885, before it arrived, a Russian force annexed the Panjdeh district north of Herat, overwhelming an Afghan force of 500.

Britain did not come to Afghanistan’s aid, though the Treaty of Gandamak required it. Amir Abdur Rahman Khan concluded he could not rely on the British against Russian aggression. Bismarck, with no direct stake, spent two decades quietly encouraging both sides — the further east Russian soldiers stood, the safer Germany’s dominance in Europe.

Finding 7 / 7

How the Game ended — and how big it really was

Traditionally the Game closes between 1895 and 1907. In September 1895 London and St Petersburg signed the Pamir Boundary Commission protocols, fixing the Afghan–Russian border by diplomacy. In August 1907 the Anglo-Russian Convention delineated control in Afghanistan, Persia and Tibet — and turned rivals into allies.

Tibet had been the last front: fearing the influence of the Russia-born Buryat Agvan Dorzhiev on the 13th Dalai Lama, Britain sent Francis Younghusband to invade in 1904 and impose the Lhasa Convention. Not everyone thinks the stakes matched the rhetoric. The historian Robert Irwin notes that in the period concerned British ruling circles “didn’t own so much as a sweetshop in Tibet”.

Why this matters in 2026

The board is crowded now, and the pieces have agency

Central Asia in 2026 is no longer a two-player board. China has overtaken Russia as the largest foreign direct investor in the region — Chinese FDI exceeding roughly USD 35 billion against about USD 20 billion from Russia — concentrated in infrastructure, logistics, energy and industry under the Belt and Road Initiative.

European actors have stepped up too: on 23 June 2026 Kazakh president Kassym-Jomart Tokayev travelled to Brussels to deepen the partnership with the EU across critical raw materials, energy, transport, digital connectivity and investment. The difference from Hopkirk’s century is the local governments: they have avoided aligning with any single outside power, courting China, Russia, the EU, the US and the Gulf at once.

“They [Afghans] can see how our history has repeated itself… Only if we understand our history can we take steps to break the cycle.”

— Mohammad Najibullah, Afghanistan’s last Soviet-backed president, on why he was translating Hopkirk’s book into Pashto while sheltering in Kabul’s UN compound. The Taliban seized and murdered him in 1996; the trail of his translation goes cold in India.

One thing to carry: the Great Game was played mostly by young officers on horseback with maps, letters and guesswork — and the guesses hardened into policy long before anyone checked them.
