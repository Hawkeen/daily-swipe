Episode of the day · 🎧

If Cows Had Litters

Why your hamburger got so pricey — the cattle cycle, a shrinking herd, imports, and four companies that pack most of America’s beef.

Freakonomics Radio · No. 689 “Here’s Why Your Hamburger Just Got So Pricey” · 2 October 2026
Host Stephen J. Dubner · guests Derrell Peel (Oklahoma State), Robert Hodgen (King Ranch), Austin Frerick (Yale), Roger Horowitz (Hagley) · ~6 min read

5 / 16 →
The hook

Ground beef: just over $7 a pound — up about 50% in five years.

That was the May 2026 average, rising faster than general inflation. The average American adult eats about 60 pounds of beef a year, roughly four times the global average — and demand, King Ranch’s CEO says, is at a 40-year high.

6 / 16 →
The core idea

Supply, not demand, sets the price

The episode’s answer is mostly biological and structural: the cattle cycle, a herd that takes years to rebuild, and a growing reliance on imports.

The hotter question is how much the concentrated meatpacking industry adds — and there the guests sharply disagree.

7 / 16 →
Finding 1 / 7

A burger is a blend

Ground beef must legally be at least 70% lean. Fed cattle — about 85% of production, finished in feedlots at 15–18 months — yield trimmings that are only about 50% lean, so they are blended with lean meat from cull cows and bulls, the other 15%.

Derrell Peel’s recipe: seven pounds of 90% lean plus one pound of 50% lean makes 85/15, a fast-food standard. Ground beef is about 47–48% of all beef eaten.

8 / 16 →
Finding 2 / 7

A ten-year cycle, 140 years old

US beef has cycled roughly every ten years for about 140 years. When prices fall, ranchers sell cows, adding supply and deepening the slump. When prices rise, rebuilding is slow: a cow bears one calf at a time, and heifers must be held back from slaughter to breed.

Beef-cow slaughter is down more than 40% over four years. “We don’t change the American cow herd in months, we change it over a period of years,” says Robert Hodgen.

9 / 16 →
Finding 3 / 7

A herd at its low point

In July there were 28.5 million beef cows in the US, far below the 1975 peak. The USDA rates more than half of US pasture and rangeland as poor or very poor.

The country has lost more than 100,000 beef farms and ranches — about 15% — mostly small operators with 25–30 cows. Hodgen thinks the industry is at or near the bottom, and prices may get worse before they get better.

10 / 16 →
Finding 4 / 7

Imports fill the gap

Imports supplied about 25% of the lean in ground beef over the past 20 years — and nearly 40% last year. One in every six pounds of beef Americans eat is now imported.

In August, President Trump temporarily expanded the low-tariff quota for lean trimmings by 300,000 metric tons. Brazil, with about twice as many cattle, has overtaken the US as the world’s largest beef producer.

11 / 16 →
Finding 5 / 7

The Big Four: efficiency or power?

JBS, Tyson, Cargill and National Beef handle roughly 85% of US beef processing. Peel says research shows they have some market power, used only slightly to depress cattle prices — while their scale saves 10–15 times more than that costs. Austin Frerick counters that their power is greater than at any point in history.

In July, choice beef retailed at $10.49 a pound (up from $9.69) while the wholesale price fell from $5.72 to $5.58 — though retailers also pay for labour, energy and real estate, so the gap alone proves nothing.

Finding 6 / 7

The Beef Trust, round two

Food historian Roger Horowitz traces an earlier “Beef Trust” — Armour, Swift, Cudahy, Morris and Wilson — built on refrigerated rail cars and branch houses. A 1919 FTC investigation exposed their market-sharing via Swift accountant Arthur Veeder’s records; forced divestment opened space for supermarkets.

Concentration returned in the 1980s and 1990s: the top four held about 25% in 1977, rose to 70% after mergers and closures, and hold about 85% today. “But to the firms, it’s never a monopoly,” says Horowitz.

Finding 7 / 7

Bribes, probes and settlements

In 2017 the Batista family’s holding company behind JBS admitted bribing nearly 1,900 Brazilian politicians and paid a record $3.2 billion fine. In spring 2026 acting Attorney General Todd Blanche opened a Justice Department probe of the Big Four, offering whistleblowers 15–30% of criminal penalties above $1 million; in September it widened to eight retailers, including Walmart and Costco.

Tyson agreed to pay over $80 million and JBS over $50 million to settle grocer suits, without admitting wrongdoing. “You shouldn’t be allowed to keep a dominant market position through bribery, yet they have,” says Frerick.

14 / 16 →
Inside a ranch

King Ranch: the barbell view

King Ranch covers 825,000 acres of south Texas and still employs 35 “cowboys”. It spans genetics (a 1,500-head Santa Gertrudis herd), cow-calf, a 50% stake in Cobalt Cattle — the fourth-largest US feeder — and DNA-based selection that has lifted its share of prime cattle to about 15%, against the 3–4% waiters often cite.

Hodgen calls the industry a “barbell”: cow-calf producers and retailers profitable, packers and feeders squeezed in the middle. Retailers reportedly earn 27–35% margins in the beef case.

What to do

Wait it out — or break it up?

Peel trusts markets to self-correct, though not before the next election — and warns that breaking up the packers would raise costs. Frerick wants protein lines separated so packers cannot butcher multiple species, and a ban on corporate ownership of animals.

In August the USDA announced insurance for ranchers who retain heifers, loans for new ranchers and programmes to recruit veterans into ranching.
