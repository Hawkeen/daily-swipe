I Contain Multitudes

Poetry · The self-published book that broke American verse open

Walt Whitman · Leaves of Grass · Brooklyn, self-published, 1855 · ~5 min read

Swipe →

5 / 14 →

The hook

In 1855 an unknown Brooklyn printer set his own poems in type, left his name off the title page — and put his portrait where the author’s name should be.

No publisher, no listed author, no precedent. Just twelve untitled poems and a man in work clothes, open-collared, hat at a jaunty angle. American poetry was never the same.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

Leaves of Grass traded rhyme and metre for a new music — to sing an expansive, democratic self.

Whitman is often called the father of free verse. Drawing on the sonorous cadences of the King James Bible and the rhythms of everyday speech, he built long, surging, unrhymed lines — the perfect vehicle for poems about identity, nationality and transcendence.

Finding 1 / 6 · The self-made book

No author, no publisher

The 1855 first edition carried neither the author’s nor the publisher’s name — Whitman was both. In their place, opposite the title page, sat Samuel Hollyer’s engraving of Whitman himself in work clothes and a jaunty hat: the poet as everyman, not literary gentleman.

Finding 2 / 6 · The form

The father of free verse

Whitman abandoned rhyme and regular metre for free verse — long lines and a loose, rolling rhythm. He built it from unlikely sources: the cadences of the King James Bible and the plain music of ordinary American speech.

Finding 3 / 6 · The centrepiece

“Song of Myself”

The collection’s great engine is the poem later titled “Song of Myself” — a groundbreaking manifesto that celebrates the individual and the interconnectedness of all life, inviting the reader into an expansive meditation on selfhood, nature and the American experience.

Finding 4 / 6 · The scandal

A body in the poem

The book was controversial in its time, especially for its overt sensuality — a frank celebration of the body that broke sharply with the genteel conventions of the era. To sing the democratic self, Whitman insisted, was to sing the physical self too.

Finding 5 / 6 · The blessing

Emerson’s letter — in gold

On 21 July 1855 Ralph Waldo Emerson wrote to the unknown poet: “I greet you at the beginning of a great career.” Whitman, never shy, printed the line in gold letters on the spine of the next edition — arguably the first great act of literary self-promotion.

Finding 6 / 6 · A growing book

A lifelong work in progress

Whitman never really finished Leaves of Grass. He revised and expanded it across his life through successive editions, ending with the ninth — the “deathbed” edition of 1891–92. What began as twelve poems had swelled to almost four hundred: a single book that grew with its author.

13 / 14 →

The lines everyone remembers

“Do I contradict myself? / Very well then I contradict myself, / (I am large, I contain multitudes.)”

— Walt Whitman, “Song of Myself”, Leaves of Grass

In three lines Whitman turned inconsistency into a virtue — a self big enough to hold every contradiction. It is still, more than a century and a half later, the most quoted note in American poetry.
