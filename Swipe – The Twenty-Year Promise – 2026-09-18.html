Episode of the day · 🎧

The Twenty-Year Promise

MacroVoices #548 — Dr Carly Anderson, ‘Emerging Energy Technologies Roundup’

Published 3 September 2026 · hosts Erik Townsend & Patrick Ceresna · ~6 min read

5 / 16 →

The thing that restarted nuclear power was not a reactor. It was a signature.

Somebody willing to commit to twenty years of power at a hundred dollars a megawatt-hour. Until a couple of years ago, Anderson says, nobody would sign that.

6 / 16 →
The core idea

Nuclear’s problem was never physics

Anderson — a PhD chemical engineer whose thesis was on making miniature lightning to heal wounds, now founder and managing director of Timescale Ventures — argues the constraint is time and financeability, not science. Everything she is excited about compresses a schedule: mass-produced components, smaller units, faster regulatory pathways, restarting and uprating existing plants.

Her priority order is unusual: not lowest cost first, but speed to a reference facility. Get one plant running, then build an order book, then iterate the cost down.

7 / 16 →
Finding 1 / 8

Why the hyperscalers changed everything

US energy utilities are relatively small and hyper-local — some operate two nuclear plants, some four. None of them could underwrite a fleet.

A hyperscaler can. It creates order books for a dozen plants or more, provides secure offtake, and thereby makes the project financeable. That, Anderson argues, is what opened the market and created the conditions for a nuclear renaissance — and it also creates a base from which to start exporting.

8 / 16 →
Finding 2 / 8

The world is already buying — from China

There are roughly 80 reactors under construction worldwide, and about 60 of them are Chinese designs; something like 30 Chinese plants are being built around the world right now. China will double its installed capacity within five years, installing 5–10 GW of nuclear a year — and it has a ten-year plan behind it.

India is building most of its capacity in partnership with Russian state-owned organisations. Anderson’s blunt summary: if you are not in the US, your options right now are China or Russia. The South Koreans have not been participating in a meaningful way.

9 / 16 →
Finding 3 / 8

Three markets — and only one is big

Advanced nuclear costs more at first, so it needs customers who can pay up. Townsend names three: defence, remote sites (mining camps, Alaska — today run on diesel gensets kept alive by weekly fuel flights into the Arctic), and AI data centres. Anderson agrees.

But the first two suit microreactors — under five megawatts, often one, sometimes 200 kilowatts — and run into export controls and a limited number of bases. As she puts it, you would need 1,001 one-megawatt turbines to equal one AP1000. The data-centre market is where the return is.

Finding 4 / 8
Image: Nuclear Regulatory Commission (US), CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons. NRC classification of reactor types, including small modular reactors.

The clock, not the core

A standard plant is a 1,000 MW chunk: site works, concrete, then shipping massive metal containers to site. Vogtle ran way over schedule and budget; the best builders in the world manage five years. Anderson wants designs deployable in under two years, ideally months — because one data-centre building now goes up in about nine months, and the power has to match that timeline.

On the regulatory side she singles out the NRC’s Part 57, which creates a pathway for advanced reactors distinct from rules written specifically for light-water designs. Her estimate for an AP1000 started today: ten years, seven if lucky.

11 / 16 →
Finding 5 / 8

What Ford did for the car

Townsend draws a sharp distinction. Factory production — building off-site rather than on-site — is not the point. He means mass production: assembly lines, robotics, repeatable precision, tight quality control, assembling reactors at a pace never previously considered. Nobody, he says, really got this until the last year and a half.

Anderson names Aalo Atomics at the small end and Radiant among microreactors, and adds her own emphasis: mass manufacturing shrinks the lead time on vessels, pumps, instruments and safety systems from years to months or weeks. She and Townsend both hope to see a microreactor online in 2028, maybe 2029.

12 / 16 →
Finding 6 / 8

The chokepoint is conversion

The fuel cycle runs: mine, convert, enrich, deconvert, fabricate, ship. The narrow step is conversion — getting mined uranium into a form that can be enriched. There are about five facilities in the world that do it. One is in the US, and that plant is very old. A great deal of the capacity sits in Russia.

Executive orders state the US will be off that Russian conversion supply by 2028. There is currently no alternative, and multiple companies are racing to fill the gap. Aalo’s appeal, for Anderson, is partly that it runs on low-enriched uranium — the same fuel as the 90 to 100 reactors already operating in the US.

13 / 16 →
Finding 7 / 8

Lasers, and half the heat you throw away

Enrichment moved from gaseous diffusion to gas centrifuges; lasers may be next. General Matter is updating centrifuge technology; next door in Paducah, Kentucky, GLE is separating uranium-235 from 238 by exciting only one isotope with lasers. Hexium in Texas uses a different laser route starting from lithium metal, which avoids the fluorine chemistry the conventional process depends on.

And the turbine is a bigger prize than it looks: a steam turbine is only about 30% thermally efficient. Supercritical CO2 as the working fluid allows higher pressures, much smaller equipment and efficiencies up to 50% — or, bolted onto existing plant as a “bottoming cycle”, another 10–20% of output from assets already built.

Finding 8 / 8

The fusion argument they actually have

Townsend is sceptical; Anderson is not. Her timing case: most new US reactors will not come online until the early-to-mid 2030s anyway — the same window in which she expects the first fusion power plants. Her cost case: with hydrogen as fuel you delete uranium mining, conversion, enrichment, decommissioning and storage from the bill.

What changed is components. High-temperature superconducting magnets shrink a fusion device from the size of a football stadium to roughly a house — that is how Commonwealth Fusion Systems made tokamaks small. Xcimer Energy in Denver took lasers from the Star Wars programme and finished the job. Townsend counters that tritium trades at $30 million per kilogram; Anderson answers with the lithium “blanket” that breeds its own tritium, leaving deuterium — found in seawater — as the only input.

15 / 16 →
Why this matters in 2026

One megawatt into a single cabinet

The scale numbers in this episode are the reason the rest of it matters. A 40-megawatt data centre is, as somebody described it to Anderson, the size of six Sam’s Clubs. NVIDIA’s roadmap points to 800-volt distribution inside these buildings, and the industry is now designing to push one megawatt — roughly the power of 1,000 homes — into a single cabinet.

Anderson expects data-centre demand to double between now and 2030. Meanwhile the grid wants a smooth, predictable load, and AI training runs swing from 80% to 20% on a one-second timescale. For comparison: the US installed six gigawatts of natural gas last year. Getting six gigawatts of new nuclear deployed, on her arithmetic, lands in the early 2030s.

The line to remember

“You really do need somebody who’s willing to sign a 20-year PPA for $100 per megawatt hour, and that didn’t exist until a couple of years ago.”

— Dr Carly Anderson, MacroVoices Episode 548, recorded 3 September 2026.

What to carry forward: Deep tech became investable, she argues, when simulation got fast enough to design hardware in hours instead of years — and when engineers out of SpaceX, Rivian and Tesla decided hardware startups were worth joining. The nuclear story is really a manufacturing-and-financing story wearing a physics costume.
