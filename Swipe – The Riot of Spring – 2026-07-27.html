Creative topic · 🎨 · Dance

The Rite of Spring

The ballet that started a riot — and reinvented modern dance

Choreography: Vaslav Nijinsky · Music: Igor Stravinsky · Ballets Russes · 1913 · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 14 →

The hook

A ballet so shocking the audience started throwing punches before it was over.

On 29 May 1913, Diaghilev’s Ballets Russes premiered The Rite of Spring at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées in Paris. Within minutes the theatre dissolved into chaos — and dance was never the same.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

Nijinsky threw out the grammar of classical ballet to stage something primal.

Subtitled “Pictures of Pagan Russia in Two Parts,” the ballet depicts a primitive tribe celebrating spring — by having a chosen young girl dance herself to death. To tell it, Nijinsky abandoned almost every idiom of classical ballet.

Finding 1 / 6 · The subject

Pagan Russia, not a fairy tale

Designer Nicholas Roerich dove into Slavic folklore and ancient ritual, turning the elegant Parisian theatre into a prehistoric Russia of earth tones and heavy tunics. This was not the swans-and-princes world Parisian audiences expected from ballet — it was a fertility rite ending in human sacrifice.

Finding 2 / 6 · The anti-ballet

Turned in, not turned out

Where ballet turns the feet outward for grace, Nijinsky turned them inward as far as possible. Dancers stamped, hunched and jerked in angular, almost violent movements. Marie Rambert, who worked with Nijinsky, recalled that all that foot-stamping was meant to represent softening the earth for fertility.

Finding 3 / 6 · The riot

Chaos at the Champs-Élysées

It began with isolated laughter and grew into full-on chaos: opposing factions bickered, Diaghilev flicked the house lights on and off, and the police had to drag some forty troublemakers out. Many thought the score and choreography seemed like the work of a madman — though anti-Russian, anti-Diaghilev factions had come determined to disrupt it.

Finding 4 / 6 · The team

Four revolutionaries, one night

The Rite was a collision of talents: Igor Stravinsky’s pounding, dissonant score; Vaslav Nijinsky’s radical choreography; Nicholas Roerich’s folkloric designs; and Sergei Diaghilev’s Ballets Russes as the daring stage, with Pierre Monteux conducting the premiere.

Finding 5 / 6 · The lost dance, refound

Sixteen years to reconstruct it

Nijinsky’s original choreography was performed only a handful of times, then lost. Scholar Millicent Hodson spent sixteen years piecing it back together from old notes and drawings, Stravinsky’s rehearsal score, photographs and conversations with people like Rambert. On 30 September 1987 the Joffrey Ballet premiered her reconstruction in Los Angeles.

Finding 6 / 6 · The legacy

From scandal to masterpiece

What the 1913 audience heard as madness is now regarded as a path-breaking twentieth-century masterpiece. The Rite of Spring is a fixture of the concert hall and has been re-choreographed by many of the great names in dance — each generation staging its own version of the sacrifice.

13 / 14 →

The takeaway

“All that foot-stamping was supposed to represent softening the earth for fertility.”

— Marie Rambert, who worked with Nijinsky

The Rite proved that dance could be ugly, violent and modern — and still be art. The riot was the birth cry of twentieth-century choreography: after 1913, ballet no longer had to be beautiful to matter.
