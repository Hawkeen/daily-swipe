Creative topic · 🎨 Music

The Oldest Opera Still Sung

Mantua, Carnival, 4 February 1607. Not the first opera — the first one that worked.

Claudio Monteverdi · L’Orfeo (La favola d’Orfeo), SV 318
Libretto by Alessandro Striggio · score published Venice, 1609 · ~6 min read

5 / 15 →

Three operas came before it. Two survive complete. Neither is in the repertory.

L’Orfeo is the earliest opera that is still regularly performed — four hundred and nineteen years on.

6 / 15 →
The core idea

The work that took a genre out of the laboratory

By the early 17th century the traditional intermedio — a musical sequence between the acts of a spoken play — was evolving into a complete musical drama. Monteverdi’s L’Orfeo moved that process out of its experimental era and provided the first fully developed example of the new genre.

Composed at the hinge between Renaissance and Baroque, it employs all the resources then known within the art of music, with a particularly daring use of polyphony — and it put the emphasis squarely on music, establishing the basic form European opera would keep for the next three hundred years.

Finding 1 / 7

What came first, and why it vanished

Jacopo Peri’s Dafne, composed with Jacopo Corsi and performed in Florence in 1598, is generally recognised as the first work belonging to the genre. It combined madrigal singing and monody with dancing and instrumental passages into a dramatic whole. Only fragments of its music still exist.

The Florentine circle around Corsi — successors to the renowned Camerata — produced more: Emilio de’ Cavalieri’s Rappresentatione di Anima, et di Corpo, and two settings titled Euridice, by Peri and by Giulio Caccini. Those survive complete, and were the direct precursors of L’Orfeo: the first musical treatments of the Orpheus myth as told in Ovid.

Finding 2 / 7

A duke goes to a wedding

On 6 October 1600, visiting Florence for the wedding of Maria de’ Medici to Henry IV of France, Duke Vincenzo Gonzaga of Mantua attended the premiere of Peri’s Euridice. His principal musicians, Monteverdi very likely among them, were probably there too.

The Duke quickly recognised the novelty of the new form — and its potential to bring prestige to whoever sponsored it. Monteverdi, born in Cremona in 1567, had joined the Mantuan court around 1590 as a viola player; through ability and hard work he became Gonzaga’s maestro della musica in 1601.

Finding 3 / 7

Thirteen lines of Ovid

The librettist was Alessandro Striggio — a young lawyer and career diplomat at the Gonzaga court, himself a talented musician, and a member of Mantua’s exclusive intellectual society, the Accademia degli Invaghiti, which was the city’s chief outlet for theatrical works.

His sources were Books 10 and 11 of Ovid’s Metamorphoses and Book 4 of Virgil’s Georgics. They gave him the material but not the structure: the events of acts 1 and 2 are covered by a mere thirteen lines in the Metamorphoses. For dramatic form he drew on Poliziano’s 1480 play, Guarini’s Il pastor fido, and Rinuccini’s libretto for Peri.

Finding 4 / 7

Two endings, and a room too small

The libretto printed in Mantua in 1607 to coincide with the premiere keeps Striggio’s ambiguous close: the Bacchantes threaten Orfeo’s destruction, but his actual fate is left in doubt — a muted version of the myth’s bloody finale. Rinuccini, writing for a Medici wedding, had been obliged to supply a happy ending; Striggio, not writing for a formal celebration, could stay closer to the myth.

Monteverdi’s score, published in Venice in 1609 by Ricciardo Amadino, resolves it entirely differently: Apollo carries Orpheus to the heavens. The musicologist Nino Pirrotta argues the Apollo ending was always the plan, but the small room hosting the premiere could not hold the theatrical machinery it required.

Finding 5 / 7

The orchestra is the scenery

In his published score Monteverdi lists around 41 instruments, with distinct groups used to depict particular scenes and characters. Strings, harpsichords, harp, organs, recorders and chitarroni represent the pastoral fields of Thrace with their nymphs and shepherds; the heavy brass — trombones, trumpets, cornetts — illustrates the underworld and its denizens.

But the work is not orchestrated as such. Monteverdi states the requirements without specifying their exact usage: in the Renaissance tradition instrumentalists followed general instructions with considerable freedom to improvise. As Nikolaus Harnoncourt points out, the players would all have been composers, and would have expected to collaborate creatively at each performance rather than play a set text.

Finding 6 / 7

A fanfare, and an early leitmotiv

The opera opens with a martial-sounding toccata for trumpets, repeated twice. Such flourishes were the standard signal for the start of a performance at the Mantuan court — a salute to the Duke; the same fanfare opens Monteverdi’s 1610 Vespers. Hans Redlich calls the sound on period instruments “shattering”.

As the brass fades, a gentler strings ritornello introduces La musica’s prologue, returning in shortened form between each of its five verses. Its function in the work as a whole is to represent the power of music — and it comes back at the end of act 2 and again at the start of act 5, one of the earliest examples of an operatic leitmotiv. It is temporally structured as a palindrome.

Finding 7 / 7

Lost for two and a half centuries

After Monteverdi’s death in 1643 the opera went unperformed for many years. It was still admired across Italy in the 1650s, but was then forgotten — as, largely, was Monteverdi — until a revival of interest in the late 19th century. In 1881 Robert Eitner published a truncated score in Berlin, intended for study rather than performance.

In 1904 Vincent d’Indy produced a French edition of parts of the work, and on 25 February 1904 his Schola Cantorum gave the first public performance in two and a half centuries. Romain Rolland, who was there, commended d’Indy for returning the opera “to the beauty it once had, freeing it from the clumsy restorations which have disfigured it”. The first modern staging followed at the Théâtre Réjane, Paris, on 2 May 1911.

Why this matters in 2026

It is being staged in three countries this season

The 2007 quatercentenary was celebrated with performances worldwide, and the work has not gone quiet since. At Glyndebourne, L’Orfeo ran 14 June – 25 July 2026 in a new staging by the Olivier Award-winning director William Kentridge.

In Italy, the Sabbioneta Chamber Opera Festival opened its concert season with L’Orfeo on 19 September 2026 at the Teatro all’Antica, with a second performance on 20 September at Villa Pisani Bonetti in Bagnolo di Lonigo. In Chicago, Haymarket Opera Company and The Newberry Consort stage it 24–27 September 2026 at DePaul University — following their 2025 presentation of Peri’s Euridice, the opera that started it.

“He blended the entire stock of newest and older possibilities into a unity that was indeed new.”

— Nikolaus Harnoncourt on Monteverdi’s first opera score. The aria, the strophic song, recitative, choruses, dances and dramatic interludes were none of them his invention.

One thing to carry: originality here is not new parts but a new assembly — and because the score leaves so much to the players, every performance of L’Orfeo is, as Mark Ringer puts it, a unique experience rather than a reading of a fixed text.
