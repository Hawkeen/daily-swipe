Book of the day · 📖

A World of Surprising Resemblances

Why did Europe industrialise and China didn’t — when, in 1750, they looked so alike?

Kenneth Pomeranz · The Great Divergence: China, Europe, and the Making of the Modern World Economy · Princeton UP · 2000 · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 15 →

The hook

What if Europe’s rise was less a triumph of superior culture and more an accident of geology and geography?

Pomeranz takes a sledgehammer to the old story of European exceptionalism. As late as 1750, he argues, the most advanced parts of Europe and Asia stood on remarkably similar ground.

6 / 15 →

The core idea

The most developed regions of Europe and Asia were comparably rich in 1750 — and hitting the same ecological ceiling.

Only after 1800 did Britain pull decisively ahead, thanks to two lucky endowments outside the price system: abundant, well-placed coal, and colonies in the New World that supplied the land-intensive goods a crowded Europe could no longer grow for itself.

7 / 15 →
Finding 1 / 6 · No head start

Compare like with like

Pomeranz insists on comparing the most advanced region of Europe — Britain — with the most advanced region of China, the Yangzi delta. On that basis the two were alike in life expectancy, consumption, and the sophistication of their markets. There was no obvious European lead to explain.

8 / 15 →
Finding 2 / 6 · Living standards

Similar lives, similar markets

Around 1750, parallels between Western Europe and East Asia ran high: comparable life expectancy, levels of commercial development, well-functioning product and factor markets, and a similar appetite among households for new consumer goods. The ‘great divergence’ had not yet begun.

9 / 15 →
Finding 3 / 6 · Shared cores

A club of advanced regions

The most dynamic cores of the eighteenth-century world — the Yangzi delta, the Kanto plain around Edo, Britain and the Netherlands — resembled one another more than each resembled its own hinterland. Advanced commercial society was not a uniquely European achievement.

10 / 15 →
Finding 4 / 6 · The ceiling

Everyone was running out of land

By 1800 the advanced Old World cores all faced the same squeeze: shortages of the land-intensive products — timber, fibre, food, fuel — that a growing economy devours. China’s cores were no worse off ecologically than Europe’s. Both were nearing a Malthusian wall.

11 / 15 →
Finding 5 / 6 · Coal

Britain’s coal was in the right place

One decisive break was geological luck: Britain’s coal lay close to its centres of production and population, making it cheap to switch from scarce timber to fossil energy. China had coal too, but much of it sat far from the dynamic Yangzi delta — a matter of location, not virtue.

12 / 15 →
Finding 6 / 6 · Colonies

The New World’s ‘ghost acres’

The second break was the Americas. Sugar, cotton and timber from slave-worked plantations gave Britain vast ‘ghost acres’ — land it never had to find at home. Pomeranz estimates that growing those imports in Europe would have swallowed 10–15 million acres, roughly two-thirds of England’s arable land.

13 / 15 →
Why this matters in 2026

The convergence question, reopened

Pomeranz’s book reframes today’s central economic question: is China simply re-converging to where it stood before 1800? In Q2 2026, China posted 4.3% GDP growth — its slowest quarterly reading since the end of 2022, below the ~4.5% forecast — even as exports jumped 27% in June. Whether the gap closes or freezes is the Great Divergence, run in reverse.

14 / 15 →

The takeaway

The advanced cores — the Yangzi delta, the Kanto plain, Britain and the Netherlands — “shared some crucial features with each other, which they did not share with the rest of the continent or subcontinent around them.”

— Kenneth Pomeranz, The Great Divergence

The provocation: the Industrial Revolution may owe less to European genius than to coal seams and colonies. Take those two accidents away, and the modern world might have looked very different.
