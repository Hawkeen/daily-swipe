The Grayscale Gaze

Visual art · Amy Sherald paints Black America in grey — so you meet the person before the label

Amy Sherald · ‘American Sublime’ retrospective (SFMOMA → Whitney → High Museum) · ~40 paintings, 2007–2024 · ~5 min read

Swipe →

The hook

What if you painted your subjects’ skin in grey — so the first thing a viewer sees isn’t their race?

That is Amy Sherald’s signature move. Her sitters — ordinary Black Americans, and a First Lady — glow against flat colour, their skin rendered in grisaille, a grey monochrome borrowed from the old masters.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

Sherald calls herself an American realist — and uses grey skin to lift race out of the first read and put individual humanity in its place.

She employs grisaille to de-emphasise the often-politicised role of race in her paintings, and by extension in her subjects’ lives — wanting viewers to ‘have an experience that was not about race first’.

Finding 1 / 5 · The lineage

An American realist

Sherald places herself in a distinctly American line: ‘I consider myself an American realist. Edward Hopper and Andy Wyeth, they’re telling these American stories, and I’m also telling American stories.’ Her subjects, in her words, are ‘American people. Black people doing stuff.’

Finding 2 / 5 · The technique

Skin in grisaille

The grey skin is a deliberate craft choice, studied with painter Odd Nerdrum in the summer of 2008. Against saturated, flat backgrounds, the grisaille figures seem to hover — quiet, monumental, refusing to be read as a colour first.

Finding 3 / 5 · The First Lady

Michelle Obama, 2018

Her official National Portrait Gallery portrait made her nationally known: Michelle Obama in a billowing patchwork dress modelled on a quilt from the Gee’s Bend quiltmakers, set against a soft, sky-blue ground. A quiet break with the conventions of official portraiture.

Finding 4 / 5 · The memorial

Breonna Taylor, 2020

For a Vanity Fair cover, Sherald painted Breonna Taylor — the portrait designed with Taylor’s mother, Tamika Palmer. The 26-year-old ER technician stands in a teal gown, a ring on her finger, ‘immortalized, unharmed, and defiant’.

Finding 5 / 5 · The flashpoint

Trans Forming Liberty, 2024

One recent work depicts a Black trans woman in a royal-blue dress and hot-pink hair, carrying a flower-filled torch and posing with the regality of the Statue of Liberty. It would become the centre of a fight over what a national museum is willing to show.

Why this matters in 2026

A retrospective, and a stand

‘American Sublime’ — about 40 paintings from 2007–2024 — debuted at SFMOMA and travelled to the Whitney, Sherald’s New York solo debut. She then withdrew the show from the Smithsonian’s National Portrait Gallery, citing a ‘culture of censorship’ after concerns over Trans Forming Liberty. The retrospective is on now at the High Museum, Atlanta (15 May – 27 Sept 2026).

The takeaway

“I consider myself an American realist. Edward Hopper and Andy Wyeth, they’re telling these American stories, and I’m also telling American stories.”

— Amy Sherald. The grey is not a gimmick but an argument: paint the person first, and let the American story hold everyone in the same light.
