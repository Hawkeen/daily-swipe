Book of the day · 📖

The Home-Run Business

Most venture bets fail — so why is VC one of the most powerful forces in the modern economy?

Sebastian Mallaby · The Power Law · 2022 · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 14 →

The hook

If most of your investments lose money, how can you run the world?

Because returns in venture capital don’t cluster around an average. They follow a power law: a tiny handful of bets succeed so enormously that they more than pay for every failure — and reshape the economy in the process.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

A few gigantic winners outweigh everything else — and that skew drives Silicon Valley and the wider tech world.

Mallaby’s history of venture capital argues that the extreme ratio of success to failure is not a bug but the whole logic of the business. One or two investments in a fund can account for more than 90% of its returns.

7 / 14 →
Finding 1 / 5 · The skew

The 90% comes from the 1%

Because outcomes are so lopsided, just one or two ‘home runs’ in a portfolio can produce the vast majority of a fund’s gains. The average deal is irrelevant; only the extremes matter.

8 / 14 →
Finding 2 / 5 · The strategy

‘Go big, always’

Since so many investments fail, VCs must swing for grand slams — ‘unicorns’ valued at $1 billion or more — rather than safe singles and doubles. Mallaby’s lesson: the best way to manage power-law risk is to embrace it, not hedge it.

9 / 14 →
Finding 3 / 5 · Smart money

Capital that rolls up its sleeves

The best venture capitalists are not passive check-writers. They supply operational and technical expertise and open their networks — helping startups raise further rounds, hire, and reach customers. The value is in the involvement, not just the money.

10 / 14 →
Finding 4 / 5 · The blind spots

The cult of the lone genius

The relentless hunt for grand slams breeds an obsession with the solitary founder-genius. Making outsized bets on unproven talent reinforces bias — and women and minorities remain represented at woefully low levels in the industry.

11 / 14 →
Finding 5 / 5 · The engine

Discovery by trial and error

Venture capital, Mallaby argues, is a machine for financing improbable ideas that no rational, risk-averse lender would touch. Reaching for the one bet that beats hundreds of others combined is precisely how the future gets funded.

12 / 14 →
Why this matters in 2026

The power law, live in 2026

The 2026 AI boom is the power law made flesh. Global venture funding hit a record $510 billion in the first half of 2026 (more than all of 2025). OpenAI and Anthropic alone drew $217 billion — 43% of all startup funding — and roughly 80% of the quarter’s VC went to AI. Rarely has capital been so concentrated in so few winners.

13 / 14 →

The takeaway

“…the power law that drives the VC business, all of Silicon Valley, the wider tech sector, and, by extension, the world.”

— Sebastian Mallaby, The Power Law. A handful of extreme successes fund a vast field of failure — and, Mallaby argues, quietly shape the future the rest of us live in.
