Book of the day · 📖

The Class War Inside the Trade War

Trade Wars Are Class Wars: How Rising Inequality Distorts the Global Economy and Threatens International Peace

Matthew C. Klein & Michael Pettis
Yale University Press, 2020 · 288 pages · ~6 min read

5 / 15 →

What if the trade war with China has almost nothing to do with China — and everything to do with who gets paid inside it?

Klein and Pettis argue that trade wars are not caused by irreconcilable national characters or interests at all. They are caused by inequality within nations.

6 / 15 →
The core argument

Distributional conflict, exported

Rising inequality means an ever-larger portion of income goes to the rich. Because the rich have a higher propensity to save than the non-rich, savings grow excessively while consumer spending shrinks dangerously.

Production then rises faster than consumption. The resulting glut has to be dumped beyond national borders — and that is the moment a domestic class conflict becomes an international trade conflict.

Finding 1 / 7
Image: Unsplash, dedicated to the public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

The glut has to go somewhere

Rising inequality shifts income towards people who save rather than spend. As consumer spending shrinks and production keeps rising, a commodity glut appears.

It must be pushed across borders. Nothing about this requires anyone to be a bad actor abroad; it is arithmetic about who inside a country receives the income.

8 / 15 →
Finding 2 / 7

Every surplus is somebody else’s deficit

The excess of global savings is driven chiefly by countries that run trade surpluses, such as China.

By definition those savings have to be absorbed somewhere. The mirror image of a surplus economy is a deficit economy that borrows and buys — which is why the two sides argue about the same imbalance from opposite ends.

9 / 15 →
Finding 3 / 7

China’s missing consumers

The authors’ central exhibit: “the share of Chinese GDP consumed by Chinese households fell by 15 percentage points between the late 1980s and the bottom in 2010.”

That is possibly the lowest household share of GDP ever seen in history — it made it impossible for Chinese workers and households to consume a significant portion of what they were producing.

10 / 15 →
Finding 4 / 7

Not culture. Policy.

The drag on consumption from inequality is exacerbated by government policies — again, such as those in China — directly intended to enrich elites by taking purchasing power away from ordinary people.

That is what makes it a class war rather than a national trait: the suppressed consumer is a policy outcome, not a cultural preference for thrift.

11 / 15 →
Finding 5 / 7

Germany did the same thing

The book traces how post-reunification wage suppression and fiscal austerity shifted income from workers to capital owners, producing a massive current account surplus.

Peter Hartz personifies the class-war hypothesis as the mastermind behind welfare cuts — though the authors caution that “the impact of Hartz IV itself is often overstated”. The Hartz IV reforms introduced by Chancellor Gerhard Schröder in 2003 contributed to cuts in welfare benefits and stagnating real wages.

12 / 15 →
Finding 6 / 7

Everybody loses except the elites

The consequence of the imbalance is economic hardship for all but the elites — in surplus countries and in deficit countries alike.

Workers in the surplus economy are paid too little to buy what they make; workers in the deficit economy take on debt, or lose the job, so that the goods can be sold.

13 / 15 →
Finding 7 / 7

Which is why globalisation is hated on both sides

The increasing distress of the impoverished masses in surplus countries and the rising anxieties of the indebted and unemployed masses in deficit countries both fuel hostility to globalisation.

Reviewers read this as the book’s political punchline: the backlash is not irrational nationalism arriving from nowhere, it is the domestic cost of the imbalance finding a voice.

Why this matters in 2026

The imbalance got bigger, not smaller

China’s annual trade surplus hit a record $1.2 trillion in 2025 as overproduction and weak domestic consumption pushed low-priced goods into world markets. In June 2026 the monthly surplus came in at $125.62bn, above forecasts.

The surplus with the EU reached a fresh record — up 27% year-on-year to $32.9bn in June — and Brussels is weighing new measures to shield local industry, with an informal deadline for progress set for October 2026.

Brookings’ Eswar Prasad says China’s surplus will have “as destructive an impact on the global trading system as Trump’s tariffs”. Tariffs did not shrink the surplus — they rerouted the goods to Europe, emerging markets and third countries.

The line to remember

“The share of Chinese GDP consumed by Chinese households fell by 15 percentage points between the late 1980s and the bottom in 2010.”

— Matthew C. Klein & Michael Pettis, Trade Wars Are Class Wars

Try this: next time you read the words “trade war”, ask who inside each country is being paid too little for the surplus to exist. The answer usually explains more than the tariff schedule does.
