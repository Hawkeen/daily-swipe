Episode of the day · 🎧

Broken China

Rhodium’s Logan Wright on how China’s economic miracle shattered — and why the system that built it is now the constraint.

ChinaTalk · 30 September 2026
Host Jordan Schneider with co-host Brad Setser (Council on Foreign Relations) · guest Logan Wright (partner, Rhodium Group), author of Broken China: How the Economic Miracle Shattered and What It Means for the World · ~6 min read

5 / 17 →
The hook

“The title is not a prediction. It’s an explanation of how we got here.”

Logan Wright is not forecasting a collapse. He is describing one that has already happened — slowly, inside China’s financial system.

6 / 17 →
The core thesis

The engine became the brake

The financial system that drove two decades of Chinese growth is now what constrains it. Beijing’s realistic options, Wright argues, involve much slower growth in the short term and a restructuring of the fiscal and financial systems.

Property, exports, AI and youth jobs are all symptoms of that single underlying problem.

7 / 17 →
Finding 1 / 7

A credit boom without precedent

After the global financial crisis, China added a third of global GDP to its bank assets in just eight years.

Then the engine slowed: credit growth fell from about 18% a year (2007–2016) to about 9% (2017–2024), and is now around 5%.

8 / 17 →
Finding 2 / 7

Zombies kept alive

The 2016 deleveraging campaign, intensified in 2018, exposed an overextended shadow-banking sector.

Rather than write down losses, the authorities kept zombie state producers and local government financing vehicles (LGFVs) alive — deferring the reckoning instead of taking it.

9 / 17 →
Finding 3 / 7

Property: the proximate cause

At its peak property was 20–25% of GDP. Construction has since fallen almost 80% and sales about 60%. The “cathartic moment” was 13 September 2021, when protests at Evergrande’s headquarters marked the effective default of China’s largest firm in its most important industry.

But Wright calls property a proximate cause, not the ultimate one — so further repair of the sector would do little for growth.

10 / 17 →
Finding 4 / 7

Weak demand and surging exports are one story

Industries that once served the property boom now have to sell abroad, often by cutting prices — which fuels deflation.

Setser noted Chinese exports growing about 10% year on year, roughly twice the pace of global trade. Wright’s limit: “China can grow as fast as its customers grow. It can’t grow relative to the rest of the world.”

11 / 17 →
Finding 5 / 7

“The constraint China faces is fiscal.”

The deficit is already about 9.5% of GDP — roughly $2 trillion last year — and Wright says it could rise to 15–16% without fixing the underlying system.

China needs an extra 4–5 percentage points of GDP in revenue. But broadening the tax base means taxing consumption, which clashes with growth goals — and digital payments have eroded tax collection from small service businesses.

12 / 17 →
Finding 6 / 7

New industries are too small to rescue it

Rhodium puts the strategic sectors — EVs, AI data-centre and hyperscaler build-out, advanced robotics — at about 6.5% of GDP, based on 2023 input-output tables. Wright doubts any hidden 10 trillion yuan of investment exists.

The AI build-out runs to about 930 billion yuan this year and an estimated 1.2 trillion next, against frontier-model revenue of roughly $11 billion. It leans on equity markets and does not look self-sustaining.

13 / 17 →
Finding 7 / 7

Graduates, migrants and pensions

China produces 12–13 million college graduates a year from a new labour cohort of 17–20 million; youth unemployment — and the disappearance of its statistics in 2023 — became a central concern.

Equalising rural and urban pensions for 250–300 million migrant workers would cost roughly 130,000 yuan per person, about 30% of GDP — large, but in Wright’s view financeable over time.

14 / 17 →
The politics

The Brezhnev analogy

“Decay is an easier political choice for China’s leaders,” Wright says. The 2018 financial super-regulator under Liu He has been dismantled and Party commissions now govern finance; he reports an anti-corruption probe that reportedly touched Fang Xinghai, which may chill reformers.

His analogy is the Soviet Brezhnev era. Reform depends on Xi or a successor — and a break from current patterns becomes more likely the longer the status quo persists.

15 / 17 →
The disagreement

Is the rivalry still systemic?

Wright argues there is no longer a systemic economic rivalry with China — only military and industrial-resilience threats, which are deterrable, and the rest of the world has more leverage than it uses.

Setser pushes back: systemic rivalry persists in other forms, mainly because China’s supply-chain controls currently look more potent than US demand-side restrictions — and supply-chain dominance may sustain export growth longer than expected.

16 / 17 →
Why this matters in 2026

Growth is already falling below forecasts

Rhodium initially expected 1–2.5% growth for China in 2026. Wright says it is trending well below that, likely with negative growth in the second and third quarters as investment contracts.
