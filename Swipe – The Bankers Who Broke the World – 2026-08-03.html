Book of the day · 📖

The Bankers Who Broke the World

How four central bankers and one golden rule made the Great Depression

Liaquat Ahamed · Lords of Finance · Penguin Press · 2009 · Pulitzer Prize · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 14 →

The hook

What if the Great Depression wasn’t an economic weather event — but the avoidable mistake of a handful of men who trusted a golden rule too much?

Ahamed — an investment manager turned historian — tells the 1920s and 1930s through four central bankers whose choices, he argues, set the world economy on a terrible downward spiral.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

The decisions of a small number of central bankers were the primary cause of the Great Depression.

Their effects, Ahamed writes, set the stage for the Second World War and reverberated for decades. The gold standard that all believed would provide an umbrella of stability instead “proved to be a straitjacket” — and the world economy began its terrible slide.

Finding 1 / 6 · The four

Four men, one world economy

The book follows Montagu Norman of the Bank of England, Benjamin Strong of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, Hjalmar Schacht of the Reichsbank and Émile Moreau of the Banque de France — a quartet whose private decisions steered the fate of millions.

8 / 14 →
Finding 2 / 6 · The straitjacket

Gold: umbrella turned straitjacket

All four revered the gold standard as the guarantor of monetary stability. Ahamed’s central charge is that it did the opposite: pegging currencies to gold transmitted deflation across borders and left governments unable to fight the downturn — a straitjacket, not an umbrella.

9 / 14 →
Finding 3 / 6 · Orthodoxy

A “badge of honour”

Joining the gold standard, Ahamed writes, became a “badge of honour” — a signal that a government had pledged itself to stable currency and orthodox finance. That prestige made the peg hard to abandon even as it strangled economies from the inside.

10 / 14 →
Finding 4 / 6 · Fallible men

History turned on temperament

Much of the drama is human: the enormous impact of a few individuals’ judgements, and their fallibility. Ahamed shows how personality, rivalry and error among the four shaped outcomes — a reminder that even the most technical decisions were made by imperfect people under pressure.

11 / 14 →
Finding 5 / 6 · The reckoning

From slump to catastrophe

The world economy’s slide into the Great Depression, in Ahamed’s account, flowed from these choices — and its effects set the stage for World War II and reverberated for decades. The human consequences of getting monetary policy wrong were immense.

12 / 14 →
Finding 6 / 6 · The lesson

No rule abolishes the cycle

The deeper warning is about faith in mechanical systems: the gold standard, Ahamed writes, was “incapable of preventing the sort of financial booms and busts” that remain a feature of the economic landscape. A potent reminder of the impact — and fallibility — of central bankers.

13 / 14 →

The takeaway

“The gold standard was incapable of preventing the sort of financial booms and busts that were, and continue to be, such a feature of the economic landscape.”

— Liaquat Ahamed, Lords of Finance

The book’s enduring value is its portrait of expert certainty gone wrong: sincere, capable men, wedded to a rule they believed was stability itself, presiding over its collapse.
