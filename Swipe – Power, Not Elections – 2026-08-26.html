Book of the day · 📖

Power, Not Elections

How did one man, never elected to anything, reshape New York for more than forty years?

Robert A. Caro · The Power Broker · 1974 (Pulitzer 1975) · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 13 →

The hook

Who really runs a city — the people it elects, or the man who controls the money?

Robert Moses was never elected to a single office. Yet for four decades he built the bridges, highways, parks and housing of New York, bending the lives of millions to his will. Caro’s 1,300-page biography asks how, and at what cost.

6 / 13 →

The core idea

Power — not ideals — builds cities. Denied power at the ballot box, Moses seized it another way.

Caro’s three great themes are Moses’s hunger for power, his genius for gaining it without ever being elected, and the complete reversal of how New Yorkers judged him over time. It is a study of how unaccountable power actually works.

Finding 1 / 5 · The pivot

Idealism, then the meat-axe

Image: C. M. Stieglitz, New York World-Telegram & Sun / Library of Congress. No known copyright restrictions, via Wikimedia Commons.
The young Moses was a reformer full of dreams — and was crushed by those who held real power. He drew the lesson that ideals are worthless without power, and spent the rest of his life amassing it.

8 / 13 →
Finding 2 / 5 · The machine

Power without a single vote

Moses ruled through unelected public authorities — above all the Triborough Bridge Authority. Its endless stream of toll revenue, plus his grip on contractors and labour unions, gave him leverage no mayor or governor could match.

9 / 13 →
Finding 3 / 5 · The doctrine

‘Power was what dreams needed’

For Moses, science, knowledge and brilliance were mere tools; they didn’t build highways or civil-service systems — power did. His whole career was the pursuit of the ‘executive support’ that turns a plan into concrete.

10 / 13 →
Finding 4 / 5 · The cost

Highways over neighbourhoods

Moses favoured the car over mass transit and drove expressways through dense communities. ‘When you operate in an overbuilt metropolis,’ he said, ‘you have to hack your way with a meat-axe.’ Whole neighbourhoods were uprooted in the name of progress.

11 / 13 →
Finding 5 / 5 · The turn

From hero to cautionary tale

Caro charts an about-face: the celebrated builder of parks and beaches became, in hindsight, a warning about power exercised without accountability. Fifty years on, The Power Broker remains the classic study of how a city is really made.

12 / 13 →

The takeaway

“…denied power within the normal framework of the democratic process, [he] stepped outside that framework to grasp power sufficient to shape a great city and to hold sway over the very texture of millions of lives.”

— Robert A. Caro, The Power Broker. The book endures because its subject is not one man but power itself.
