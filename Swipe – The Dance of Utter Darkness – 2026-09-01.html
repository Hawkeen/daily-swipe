The Dance of Utter Darkness

Butoh — Tatsumi Hijikata & Kazuo Ohno, Japan, from 1959

Dance · post-war Japanese avant-garde · ~6 min read

5 / 15 →

A dance that ends with a live chicken and a chase into darkness. The audience was outraged. The choreographer was banned. That is where butoh begins.

Tokyo, 1959.

6 / 15 →
The core idea

An art form that resists fixity

Butoh is a form of Japanese dance theatre that arose in 1959 through the collaboration of Tatsumi Hijikata and Kazuo Ohno, “in the protective shadow of the 1950s and 1960s avant-garde”.

It is known to “resist fixity” and is famously hard to define — Hijikata himself viewed the formalisation of butoh with “distress”. Its common features: playful and grotesque imagery, taboo topics, extreme or absurd environments, and, traditionally, white body make-up with slow, hyper-controlled motion.

Finding 1 / 7

Forbidden Colours, 1959

The first butoh piece, Kinjiki (Forbidden Colors), premiered at a dance festival in 1959, based on the Yukio Mishima novel of the same name.

It explored the taboo of homosexuality and ended with a live chicken held between the legs of Kazuo Ohno’s son Yoshito, after which Hijikata chased Yoshito off the stage in darkness. Mainly as a result of the audience outrage, Hijikata was banned from the festival — which established him as an iconoclast.

Finding 2 / 7

A name dug out of the dictionary

The earliest performances were called, in English, “Dance Experience”. In the early 1960s Hijikata used ankoku-buyou — dance of darkness — to describe his work.

Then he swapped the word. “Buyo” was thick with associations of Japanese classical dance; “butoh” was a long-discarded word for dance that originally meant European ballroom dancing. He took the deadest word available and gave it the darkest possible meaning.

Finding 3 / 7

A refusal, not a style

A key impetus was a reaction against the Japanese dance scene of the time, which Hijikata felt was overly based on imitating the West and on following traditional forms like Noh. He sought to “turn away from the Western styles of dance, ballet and modern”.

Ankoku butō was built instead on a vocabulary of “crude physical gestures and uncouth habits… a direct assault on the refinement (miyabi) and understatement (shibui) so valued in Japanese aesthetics”.

Finding 4 / 7

Genet, Artaud, kabuki — and Tōhoku

Hijikata’s vision combined the absurdity and deviancy of Genet, the mandate to “reject form and incite chaos” of Artaud’s Theatre of Cruelty, the bawdy, disreputable origins of kabuki — and the broken, bow-legged movements of the impoverished workers of his Tōhoku homeland.

Reading Mishima, Lautréamont, Artaud, Genet and Sade, he delved into grotesquerie, darkness and decay, and explored the transmutation of the human body into other forms, such as those of animals.

Finding 5 / 7

Butoh-fu: a notation for becoming something else

Hijikata developed a poetic and surreal choreographic language, butoh-fu (fu means “notation”), to help the dancer transform into other states of being.

Most butoh exercises use image work to varying degrees — from the razorblades and insects of ankoku butō to Dairakudakan’s threads and water jets. The general trend is towards the body as “being moved”, from an internal or external source, rather than consciously moving a body part.

Finding 6 / 7

The soul and the architect

In Masson-Sékiné and Viala’s Shades of Darkness, Ohno is “the soul of butoh” while Hijikata is “the architect of butoh”. Hijikata was a fearsome technician of the nervous system who shaped artists working in groups; Ohno was the more natural, individual and nurturing figure, who influenced solo artists.

Ohno made his signature solo Admiring La Argentina in 1977, at 71, directed by Hijikata — an evocation of the Spanish dancer Antonia Mercé, whose 1929 Tokyo performance had made him want to dance. He died in 2010, aged 103. His counter-formula to Hijikata’s: “Form comes of itself, only insofar as there is a spiritual content to begin with.”

Finding 7 / 7

Out of Japan — and into the news

From the early 1980s butoh experienced a renaissance as groups began performing outside Japan for the first time. The style was then marked by “full body paint (white or dark or gold), near or complete nudity, shaved heads, grotesque costumes, clawed hands, rolled-up eyes and mouths opened in silent screams”.

America learned the word through a catastrophe: during a Sankai Juku performance in which the dancers hung upside down on ropes from a tall building in Seattle, one rope broke and a performer was killed. The footage played on national news. A PBS documentary of a butoh performance in a cave, without an audience, widened the awareness further.

Why this matters in 2026

The form outlives its founders

Sankai Juku — founded in 1975 by Ushio Amagatsu, who had co-founded the seminal butoh collective Dairakudakan in 1972 — has a US tour scheduled for autumn 2026.

The company also ran its Semimaru summer butoh workshop from 1–5 August 2026, with a performance on 7 August at Colare, the Kurobe International Cultural Center in Toyama prefecture — a residential summer workshop held every year since 1987.

It does all this without its founder: Amagatsu, who introduced butoh to Western audiences, died of heart failure on 25 March 2024, aged 74. The troupe remains active.

The line to remember

“Since I believe neither in a dance teaching method nor in controlling movement, I do not teach in this manner.”

— Tatsumi Hijikata

The paradox worth keeping: both Mikami Kayo and Maro Akaji say Hijikata told his disciples not to imitate his dance when they left to found their own companies. Iwana Masaki calls present-day butoh a “tendency” rather than an inherited method — which is why there are as many types of butoh as there are butoh choreographers.
