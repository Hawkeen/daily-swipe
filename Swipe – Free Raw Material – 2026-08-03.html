Book of the day · 📖

Free Raw Material

How your experience quietly became someone else’s asset

Shoshana Zuboff · The Age of Surveillance Capitalism · PublicAffairs · 2019 · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 15 →

The hook

What if the digital age’s most valuable resource isn’t your data — but your future behaviour, sold before you act?

Zuboff argues that a new economic logic quietly took hold inside Silicon Valley: one that treats your everyday experience as raw material and turns predictions about what you will do next into a tradable product.

6 / 15 →

The core idea

Surveillance capitalism “unilaterally claims human experience as free raw material for translation into behavioural data.”

Some of that data improves the product you use. The rest — a “behavioural surplus” — is fed into prediction products and sold to business customers in what Zuboff calls behavioural futures markets. It is not, she insists, the same thing as technology, nor an inevitable one.

7 / 15 →
Finding 1 / 6 · The discovery

Behavioural surplus

Zuboff traces the model to Google’s discovery that the “data exhaust” thrown off by search — clicks, spelling, dwell times — could be repurposed to predict and target behaviour. The leftover data was no longer waste; it was surplus, and surplus was the beginning of a business.

8 / 15 →
Finding 2 / 6 · The product

Markets in your next move

The raw material is refined into predictions — what you will click, buy, or believe — and those predictions are traded in behavioural futures markets. The paradigm, Zuboff writes, seeks to convert all human experience into data in order to predict, influence and control behaviour at scale.

9 / 15 →
Finding 3 / 6 · From knowing to shaping

A new species of power

The best way to predict behaviour, Zuboff argues, is to shape it. She names the result instrumentarian power — in her words, “the power to know and shape human behaviour toward others’ ends.” The point is no longer merely to watch us, but to steer us.

10 / 15 →
Finding 4 / 6 · Nudges, not force

“Tune, herd and condition”

Left unchecked, she warns, surveillance capitalism will “tune, herd, and condition” behaviour with ever more subtle cues. Her contrast is stark: “Totalitarianism operated through the means of violence, but instrumentarian power operates through the means of behavioural modification.”

11 / 15 →
Finding 5 / 6 · Not the technology

Invented, therefore reversible

A central claim: surveillance capitalism is a specific economic model that was deliberately invented and deployed — not a natural by-product of digital technology. If it was made by choices, Zuboff argues, it can be unmade by them; the machine and the market logic behind it are not the same thing.

12 / 15 →
Finding 6 / 6 · The stakes

The cost to democracy

The threat, in Zuboff’s telling, is to human nature in the 21st century much as industrial capitalism disfigured the natural world in the 20th. Surveillance capitalism, she charges, has deepened inequality, sown societal chaos and undermined democracy — and it does so largely without our meaningful consent.

Why this matters in 2026

Inference outpaces the law

The full EU AI Act becomes applicable in August 2026, with penalties reaching €35 million or 7% of global turnover — Europe’s bid to move AI oversight from principle to obligation. Yet in the US, the FBI confirmed to Congress in March 2026 that it buys Americans’ location data from brokers, and analysts warn AI inference now runs ahead of rules written only for data collection. Zuboff’s 2019 map fits a landscape that has only sharpened.

The takeaway

“Surveillance capitalism unilaterally claims human experience as free raw material for translation into behavioural data.”

— Shoshana Zuboff, The Age of Surveillance Capitalism

The book’s gift is a name for something we sensed but couldn’t see: an economy built not on what we make or buy, but on what we can be predicted — and nudged — to do next.
