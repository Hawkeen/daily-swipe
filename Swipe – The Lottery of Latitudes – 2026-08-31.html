Book of the day · 📖

The Lottery of Latitudes

Why did some peoples end up with steel swords and ocean-going ships — and others with stone tools? The answer, one biologist argued, is written in the map.

Jared Diamond · Guns, Germs, and Steel · 1997 · Pulitzer Prize · ~6 min read

The hook

“Why do you white people have so much cargo, and we have so little?”

In 1972, on a New Guinea beach, a local politician named Yali asked Jared Diamond exactly that — why Europeans arrived with so much wealth and technology while his own people had almost none. Diamond spent the next 25 years trying to answer without any appeal to race.

6 / 14 →

The core thesis

Geography, not biology, set the course of history.

The huge gaps in wealth and power between peoples, Diamond argues, grew from differences in their environments — not from any difference in intelligence. A few regions simply held the raw materials for farming, and cities, states, writing and armies all followed from that one head start.

7 / 14 →
Finding 1 / 7 · Food first

Everything grows from the farm

Food production is the master key. Where wild plants and animals could be domesticated, farmers made surpluses — and surpluses freed people to become soldiers, priests, kings, scribes and inventors. Dense, stratified societies are impossible until someone grows the extra grain.

8 / 14 →
Finding 2 / 7 · The jackpot

Eurasia’s biological luck

The Fertile Crescent held the richest set of domesticable wild plants on Earth. And of the world’s 14 domesticated large mammals — the cow, pig, horse, sheep and the rest — 13 were native to Eurasia. That windfall of food and muscle power was pure geographic luck, not merit.

9 / 14 →
Finding 3 / 7 · The axis

Why Eurasia spread faster

Eurasia’s main axis runs east–west, so a crop or animal could travel thousands of miles through the same climate and day-length. The Americas and Africa run north–south — farming there had to cross deserts and climate zones, so it spread slowly, if at all.

10 / 14 →
Finding 4 / 7 · Anna Karenina

Why so few animals tamed

“Domesticable animals are all alike; every undomesticable animal is undomesticable in its own way.”

A species must pass every test — diet, growth rate, breeding in captivity, temperament, a social hierarchy to hijack. Fail one and it’s out. Zebras bite and won’t let go; only ~14 of 148 big candidate mammals ever made the cut.

11 / 14 →
Finding 5 / 7 · Germs

Livestock loaded the guns

Living packed together with animals bred the crowd diseases — smallpox, measles, flu — that Eurasians grew partly resistant to. Carried to the Americas, those germs killed a huge share of native peoples before the battles even began. Disease, more than weapons, did the conquering.

12 / 14 →
Finding 6 / 7 · Downstream

Guns, steel and writing

Dense farming societies could feed metalworkers, standing armies, ships and bureaucracies. The ‘guns, germs, and steel’ of the title are effects of an agricultural head start — the proximate weapons, not the ultimate cause.

13 / 14 →
Finding 7 / 7 · The pushback

Is it just determinism?

Critics charge Diamond with geographic determinism — making environment the hero and underplaying culture, ideas, institutions and human choice. His reply: he is explaining the broad shape of history, not excusing conquest or denying that people make their own decisions.

14 / 14 ✓

The takeaway

“History followed different courses for different peoples because of differences among peoples’ environments, not because of biological differences among peoples themselves.”

— Jared Diamond, Guns, Germs, and Steel. Comforting and unsettling at once: the winners of history were, more than anything, lucky in their latitudes.
