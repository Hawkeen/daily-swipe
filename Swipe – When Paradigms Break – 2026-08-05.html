Book of the day · 📖

When Paradigms Break

The book that taught us science doesn’t march forward — it lurches

Thomas S. Kuhn · The Structure of Scientific Revolutions · University of Chicago Press · 1962 · ~6 min read

Swipe →

5 / 14 →

The hook

What if scientific progress isn’t a steady climb toward truth, but a series of violent switches from one worldview to the next?

Before Kuhn, most people pictured science as knowledge piling up, brick by brick. He argued it doesn’t work like that at all.

6 / 14 →

The core idea

Science alternates between long calm and sudden rupture.

Kuhn distinguished ‘normal science’ — steady puzzle-solving inside an accepted framework — from the ‘revolutions’ that overturn that framework. A build-up of anomalies triggers a crisis, and the crisis is resolved not by more evidence alone but by a wholesale shift to a new paradigm.

7 / 14 →
Finding 1 / 6 · Normal science

Most science is ‘filling in the details’

Under a settled paradigm, scientists rarely question fundamentals. They solve puzzles — refining measurements, extending theory, tidying up loose ends. Kuhn called this ‘normal science’: productive, cumulative, and firmly bounded by the ruling framework.

8 / 14 →
Finding 2 / 6 · What a paradigm is

A shared way of seeing

A paradigm is the constellation of concepts, methods and accepted exemplars that define a field at a given time — the taken-for-granted rules of the game. It tells researchers what counts as a real problem and what a legitimate answer looks like.

9 / 14 →
Finding 3 / 6 · Anomalies

The cracks that won’t go away

Sooner or later, observations turn up that the paradigm can’t absorb — anomalies. A few can be ignored or patched. But as they mount and resist every fix, they start to eat away at the framework’s usefulness.

10 / 14 →
Finding 4 / 6 · Crisis

When confidence cracks

Enough unresolved anomalies tip a field into crisis. The old rules feel shaky; researchers improvise competing versions; the sense of a shared foundation dissolves. Crisis is the necessary precondition, Kuhn argued, for anything genuinely new.

11 / 14 →
Finding 5 / 6 · Revolution

The paradigm shift

A revolution replaces the old paradigm with a new one — Ptolemy to Copernicus, Newton to Einstein. It is less a tidy addition than a gestalt switch: afterwards, scientists see the same world through different eyes and ask different questions.

12 / 14 →
Finding 6 / 6 · Incommensurability

No neutral scorecard

Rival paradigms can be ‘incommensurable’ — there’s no wholly neutral yardstick to rank them, because each defines its own terms and problems. Choosing between them means weighing both against nature and against each other, a judgement no algorithm settles.

13 / 14 →

The takeaway

“The decision to reject one paradigm is always simultaneously the decision to accept another, and the judgment leading to that decision involves the comparison of both paradigms with nature and with each other.”

— Thomas S. Kuhn, The Structure of Scientific Revolutions

Kuhn gave us the phrase ‘paradigm shift’ — and a humbler picture of science: not a serene accumulation of facts, but a human enterprise that occasionally has to tear itself down to move on.
